01  ·  Executive summary

Seven findings, and what follows from them

There are two entirely separate questions inside “should I buy into God City?”, and almost every sales conversation about this project fuses them. The first is whether Sindhudurg is a good place to own land in 2026. The second is whether this particular vehicle, promoted by this particular entity, is a safe way to do it. This report answers them separately, because the honest answers point in different directions.

Finding 1 — The location thesis is real, and better-evidenced than most

Sindhudurg is not a speculative fiction. A functioning airport sits inside the district at Chipi; Goa's Manohar International Airport at Mopa is roughly 14 km from Sawantwadi; the district's stretch of the four-laned NH-66 is effectively complete; a Vande Bharat service runs on the Konkan Railway; and a Mumbai–Vijaydurg Ro-Ro ferry began operating on 1 March 2026. Layered on top is an unusually credible public-sector tourism pipeline — an IHCL/Taj hotel agreement at Shiroda–Velagar, India's first submarine-and-reef tourism project with the ex-INS Guldar scuttled off Nivati Rocks in May 2026, a Swadesh Darshan 2.0 destination designation, and a proposed MIDC pharma hub at Adali. None of that is marketing.

Finding 2 — But most of that value is already in the price

Mopa, Chipi, NH-66 and the ferry are delivered. They are not upside waiting to be captured; they are the reason land in the Kudal–Sawantwadi belt already trades where it does. What remains genuinely unpriced sits in the announcement tier — the 200-acre Film City at Nandos, the Chipi “Aerocity”, the ₹70,000 crore greenfield Konkan Expressway, the runway extension — and not one of those has a traceable sanctioned DPR, award or funded programme. The calibration point is the Navi Mumbai–Chipi air link: announced with a firm date of 15 August 2026, which passed with no service. Figure 4 separates the tiers.

Finding 3 — The developing entity is two years old and capitalised at ₹1,00,000

God City LLP was incorporated on 6 February 2024 with the Registrar of Companies, Kanpur, under LLPIN ACF-3634. Its registered office is in Noida, Uttar Pradesh. Its total partner contribution is ₹1,00,000 — the practical legal minimum. Figure 16 sets that against an order-of-magnitude build-up for a 150-acre township of roughly ₹389 crore. The ratio is about 1 : 389,000. This is not, on its own, an allegation: developers routinely build through project SPVs, landowner joint ventures, development-management agreements and debt. But it means some other balance sheet must be carrying this, and the buyer has not been told whose.

Finding 4 — The track record cited belongs to a different company's project

The credential published for one designated partner is “directorial experience with the WinstenPark Real Estate Project of VHR Group”. Winsten Park is a 3.71-acre, three-tower, roughly 674-unit scheme of studios, shops and IT space at Knowledge Park V, Greater Noida. Its promoter of record on UP RERA is SKV Infotech Private Limited; the brand is VHR Group, a Verma-family business established in 1986. Phase I (UPRERAPRJ5008) was launched in December 2015 with completion due 14 December 2020; Phase II (UPRERAPRJ9566) was due 7 July 2021; possession on parts of the scheme is reported into November–December 2025, and Phase III (UPRERAPRJ11430) carries a completion date of 16 January 2028. Whatever one concludes about that record, three things are certain: it is a different asset class, in a different state, under a different promoter entity — and it is not a delivery record of God City LLP, which has delivered nothing. Figure 8 draws the distinction.

Finding 5 — There is no MahaRERA registration in the public domain

This is the finding that governs all the others. Under section 3 of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act as administered by MahaRERA, registration is compulsory for any project where the land exceeds 500 square metres or the plots or apartments exceed eight, counting all phases — and each phase requires its own registration. Until registration is granted, a project may not lawfully be advertised, marketed, booked or sold. Searches of MahaRERA's project register, its lapsed-project list and its suspended/abeyance list, together with general web search, returned no registration for this project in February 2026. Meanwhile a channel-partner recruitment programme is live on the promoter's website.

Finding 6 — Almost nothing checkable has been published

Figure 7 tests sixteen disclosure items against the public record. The ten most decision-critical — registration number, contracting entity, village and survey numbers, sanctioned layout, title chain, CRZ position, ESA status, township locational clearance, price list, possession date — are all absent. What is published is a scale (~150 acres), an architect's name, two biographies and a broker programme. A conveyancing lawyer, a valuer or a lender can act on none of those. Without survey numbers, no title search, no CRZ check against the Sindhudurg Coastal Zone Management Plan and no valuation can even begin.

Finding 7 — The strongest fact in the project's favour is the master planner

The engagement of Architect Hafeez Contractor — Padma Bhushan 2016, the practice behind Hiranandani Gardens, The Imperial and The 42 — is a serious, top-tier appointment, and it is the kind of claim that can be confirmed with a single phone call to the practice. It is also the only element of the proposition that suggests real township-scale intent rather than a land-marketing exercise. It deserves to be verified rather than dismissed.

What follows from all seven

Nothing in this report says God City will fail, and nothing in it alleges wrongdoing. It says something narrower and more useful: as at February 2026 there is not yet enough in the public domain to make a decision on, and the single document that would change that costs nothing to obtain. Ask for the MahaRERA registration number. If it exists, the entire diligence programme in Figure 14 becomes worth commissioning. If it does not, no amount of further analysis — and no amount of money — should be committed until it does.

The rest of this report is the working behind those seven findings: the district and its numbers (Part II), the market and what land actually costs at each rung of the ladder (Part III), the law that governs every step (Part IV), the economics for both developer and buyer (Part V), a ranked risk register (Part VI), and a structured six-month work plan with the exact documents to request (Part VII). Part VIII gives the verdict — six of them, because a holiday-home buyer, an NRI, a broker and a co-investor are not asking the same question.

How this report was built, and how to read it

This is a public-record report. Every factual claim in it comes from a published source — a corporate register, a regulator's portal, a government or statutory instrument, a news report, a listing portal, or the promoter's own website — and the source is named either in the text, in the figure's source line, or in Appendix A. No confidential information was used, nobody was interviewed, and no site visit was made. That is a real limitation and it is stated here rather than buried.

One distinction that matters more than any other

Several striking market statistics circulating about Sindhudurg — land up 28% within 25–30 km of Mopa in eighteen months, prices up 25–30% district-wide, highway-frontage premiums of 20–30% — originate in the marketing material of firms selling plots in exactly that market. They may well be right. They are reproduced in this report where relevant because they are part of the information environment a buyer is operating in, but they are labelled Asserted every time, and no conclusion here rests on them. The same discipline is applied to the promoter's own claims.

What “not found in the public domain” means

It means exactly what it says: a search was run and nothing was returned. It is not a finding that a document does not exist. A promoter may hold a complete set of title deeds, a sanctioned layout and a pending registration application and simply not publish any of it — which is common, and not in itself improper. The correct response to an absence is therefore not suspicion; it is a written request. Section 27 of this report sets out the request in full.

Two things this report deliberately does not do

It does not value any specific parcel of land, because no parcel has been identified. And it does not tell you what to do with your money — it tells you what is known, what is not, what the law requires, and what sequence of work would close the gap. Figure 19 gives six conditional verdicts, each naming the specific document that would change it.

A note on spelling and units

“Sindhudurg” is used throughout, including where sources use “Sindhudurga” or “Sindhudurgnagari” (the district headquarters at Oros). Land areas follow local convention: 1 guntha = 1,089 sq ft ≈ 101.2 m²; 1 acre = 40 guntha = 43,560 sq ft; 1 hectare ≈ 2.471 acres. Where a source quotes a rate per guntha or per square yard, the conversion to ₹ per square foot is this report's and is shown alongside the original figure so it can be checked. Currency is Indian rupees; “lakh” is 100,000 and “crore” is 10,000,000.

Part I
The proposition, and the people behind it
Before the district, the market or the law: what exactly is being offered, by whom, on what evidence — and which parts of the story belong to somebody else.
03

What is actually being offered

Stripped of adjectives, the proposition on God City LLP's website is this. The company describes itself as a real estate development business building premium projects across India, and its flagship is God City — an integrated luxury township in what it calls the “Sindhudurg growth corridor”, envisaged to extend across approximately 150 acres, offering luxury plots, villas and studios, and master planned by Architect Hafeez Contractor. The positioning is resort-inspired coastal living combined with the structure of a master-planned community, aimed at three buyer types at once: lifestyle end-users, holiday-home buyers, and investors. A channel-partner programme invites agents to “represent premium real estate developments” and to access project briefs, pricing, availability and approved sales assets.

The company's own blog makes the market argument explicitly and, it should be said, rather well: that Sindhudurg has long been known for clean beaches, historic forts and a relaxed coastal life, that it is now gaining attention because of improving connectivity, tourism development and proximity to Goa, and that for buyers who want “the charm of Goa without the overcrowding and high entry cost of saturated beach markets”, it is a compelling alternative. That is a coherent thesis and Part II of this report largely supports it.

What is not on the website is equally specific, and is set out in full in Figure 7: no village or survey numbers, no site plan, no MahaRERA registration number, no price list, no payment schedule, no possession date, and no identification of the entity that would actually sign an agreement for sale. Every project page carries the words Coming Soon.

A fair reading

A project genuinely at pre-launch stage would look exactly like this. Land aggregation is sensitive and developers routinely keep survey numbers confidential until options are exercised; registration follows layout sanction, which follows N.A. conversion, which follows assembly. The concern raised in this report is not that the project is early. It is that marketing and channel recruitment are running ahead of registration, which is the one part of that sequence the law does not treat as optional.

Four observations follow, and none of them requires any inference beyond the record itself.

It is two years old. An entity incorporated in February 2024 has, by definition, no delivery history. That is not disqualifying — every developer starts somewhere — but it does mean the question “what have they built before?” has no answer at the entity level, and the answer offered instead belongs to somebody else (section 05).

It is capitalised at ₹1,00,000. Total partner contribution is the legal minimum in substance. A 150-acre township requires capital of a different order entirely; Figure 16 builds that figure up and puts the two side by side.

It is an Uttar Pradesh entity. Registered with RoC-Kanpur, with its office in Noida. The project is in Maharashtra, in a district whose land law — kul tenancy, Inam and Devasthan grants, Occupant Class-II tenure, the Maharashtra Private Forests (Acquisition) Act, CRZ practice under the Sindhudurg CZMP — is a local specialism that defeats experienced outsiders routinely. That is a capability question, not a character one, and it is scored as such in Figure 9.

And it may not be the entity a buyer contracts with. This is the most important of the four. Land in Maharashtra has to be held by somebody, and a MahaRERA registration names a specific legal promoter. Neither the land-holding vehicle nor the intended registered promoter is disclosed. A buyer's recourse — for delay interest, for defect, for non-delivery of amenities — runs against whatever entity signs the agreement for sale, and against its balance sheet. Not against a brand, and not necessarily against this LLP. Figure 8 draws that missing box explicitly, because it is the thing most likely to be glossed over in a sales meeting.

06

The master planner

The project states that it is master planned by Architect Hafeez Contractor. This is a genuinely significant claim and it deserves to be reported accurately rather than waved past. Hafeez Contractor founded his practice in 1982 and has led it to a team of over six hundred professionals. He was awarded the Padma Bhushan in 2016, holds two honorary doctorates, and has received more than seventy-five national and international awards. His portfolio includes Hiranandani Gardens in Powai — the reference township for master-planned living in India — along with The Imperial towers in Mumbai, The 42 in Kolkata, the D. Y. Patil Stadium in Navi Mumbai, and DLF Cyber City in Gurgaon. The New York Times called him “the man who draws India”.

If the engagement is real and current, it is the single strongest signal in the entire proposition — because a practice of that standing does not typically attach itself to a land flip, and because township master planning is precisely the discipline the project needs. It is also the most easily verified claim on the website: one enquiry to the practice confirms or refutes it, and that enquiry is task four in Figure 14.

Until it is confirmed, however, it should be held at Asserted. No scope of appointment, no concept drawing, no land-use plan and no confirmation from the practice appears in the public domain. Naming a celebrated architect is, unfortunately, one of the cheapest available forms of credibility in Indian real estate marketing, and the gap between “has been engaged to master plan” and “was consulted once” is invisible from outside.

Part II
The place
Sindhudurg on its own terms — the demography that decides who a township here can sell to, the access that has genuinely changed, and a catalyst pipeline sorted by how firm each commitment actually is.
08

Sindhudurg: the district in numbers

Sindhudurg is Maharashtra's southernmost district, part of the Konkan division, bordering Goa. It covers 5,207 km² across eight talukas — Devgad, Kankavli, Vaibhavwadi, Malvan, Kudal, Vengurla, Sawantwadi and Dodamarg — containing 748 villages and 8 towns, administered from three subdivisions (Kankavli, Kudal, Sawantwadi) with the district headquarters at Oros, the planned town also called Sindhudurgnagari. It was India's first district to be formally designated a tourism district, and it is a Swadesh Darshan 2.0 destination.

Why the demography matters commercially

A population of about 850,000 at a density of 163 per km² makes this one of Maharashtra's least crowded districts. Literacy at 85.6% runs above the state's 82.3%. And the sex ratio of 1,036 women per 1,000 men — far above the state's 929 — is the classic demographic fingerprint of sustained out-migration of working-age men, historically to Mumbai and increasingly to Goa, where the MIDC's own pharma-hub proposal notes that local graduates leave for employment.

For a township developer that has a specific consequence. Local end-user demand for premium housing is thin. A 150-acre luxury scheme here is not selling to Sindhudurg; it is selling to Mumbai and Pune, with some NRI interest. Its absorption therefore tracks sentiment in those markets, not conditions in the district — which makes it more cyclical, more sentiment-driven and considerably less liquid than the same product in a city with organic end-user depth. It also means resale depends on finding another outside buyer with the same thesis, which is the mechanism behind risk R9 in Figure 10.

Why the rainfall matters practically

At roughly 4,608 mm a year, Sindhudurg receives around three times Mumbai's annual rainfall, concentrated in June to September. Two consequences follow that rarely appear in a brochure. First, earthworks, roads, drainage and services compress into a seven-month season, which makes a twelve-month construction programme arithmetically impossible and is a standing explanation for Konkan schedule slippage. Second, the district then runs short of water in April and May — so a buyer who inspects during the pleasant dry months sees neither how the site drains under monsoon load nor how it performs under water stress. Insist on at least one monsoon-season site visit before committing. There is no substitute for watching where the water goes.

The district also sits inside a genuine hazard envelope: Western Ghats landslide susceptibility, coastal flood exposure, and an Arabian Sea cyclone frequency that has risen in recent years — Cyclone Tauktae in May 2021 was the fifth-strongest Arabian Sea storm since 1998. Maharashtra's own Konkan Disaster Mitigation Project covers Sindhudurg among five coastal districts, funding saline embankments, anti-sea-erosion bunds and landslide protection. This is a manageable risk, correctly priced; it is not a reason to avoid the district. It is a reason to read a geotechnical report before buying a slope.

Orientation: where the corridor actually is

The geography that matters for this proposition is a narrow inland belt running from Kankavli south through Kudal and Oros to Sawantwadi and Dodamarg, roughly parallel to the coast and threaded by both NH-66 and the Konkan Railway. That belt is where the airports, the stations, the highway and the Goa border converge; it is also where the land is buildable without colliding immediately with the Coastal Regulation Zone. Almost every project marketed as “Sindhudurg, near Goa” sits along it.

Two boundaries frame it. To the west, the coast — Devgad, Achara, Malvan and Tarkarli, Vengurla, Shiroda — carries the scenery, the tourism and the price premium, and also the 200 m No-Development Zone under CRZ-III B. To the east, the Western Ghats rise into ecologically sensitive terrain where the Bombay High Court has directed notification of a 25-village Sawantwadi–Dodamarg wildlife corridor. The developable middle is genuinely attractive and genuinely constrained on both sides, which is precisely why the exact survey numbers of any 150-acre assembly are not a detail — they are the whole question.

09

Access — what has genuinely changed

Sindhudurg's investment case is, almost entirely, a time-compression case: the argument that a place which used to be twelve hours from Mumbai is now six, and that value follows. Three of the four access channels have genuinely improved. One has not, and it happens to be the one that would matter most to a weekend-home buyer.

Road — the four-laning of NH-66

The 466 km Panvel–Sindhudurg corridor is the backbone of the thesis, and within Sindhudurg it is substantially finished: packages P-9 and P-10 are reported at around 99% complete and the district section is effectively done. The full corridor is targeted for completion around the end of 2026 or early 2027, which would cut Mumbai–Goa from twelve or thirteen hours to about six. The caveat is the history: this project has been delayed repeatedly for a decade by land acquisition, environmental clearances and design changes, and “by December” has been said before. For Sindhudurg specifically, though, the benefit is already largely banked.

Sea — the Mumbai–Vijaydurg Ro-Ro

A genuinely new channel. The Ro-Pax vessel M2M Princess began operating from Bhaucha Dhakka (Ferry Wharf) to Vijaydurg on 1 March 2026, cutting the journey to roughly six to seven hours against twelve by road, with sixteen trips planned in the first month. Fares were set at ₹2,500 economy and ₹4,000 premium economy, with ₹1,000 for a two-wheeler and ₹6,000 for a car; the inaugural sailing carried about 390 passengers and 21 cars. Two qualifications: Vijaydurg is in Devgad taluka at the district's northern end, a substantial onward drive from the Kudal–Sawantwadi belt, and a single-vessel seasonal service on the Arabian Sea is not year-round infrastructure. It is a real improvement and a fragile one.

Rail — Konkan Railway

Four stations serve the district — Kankavli, Sindhudurg, Kudal and Sawantwadi — and the Mumbai CSMT–Madgaon Vande Bharat has run since June 2023. Electrification is progressing, with a reported ₹1.58 billion package awarded in June 2026. Doubling is a different matter: it is planned, contested by the Save Konkan Ecology Forum on environmental and displacement grounds, and should not be assumed on any particular timetable.

Air — the gap in the story

This is where a careful reader should slow down. Sindhudurg Airport at Chipi (SDW) is open and growing fast in percentage terms — but from a very low base.

In FY 2025-26 the airport handled 73,022 passengers — up 398.48% — across 1,566 aircraft movements, up 200%. Those growth rates are real and they are quoted everywhere. The absolute numbers are equally real: roughly 200 passengers a day, about three flights, operated by a single carrier, Fly91, to three cities — Pune, Hyderabad and Bengaluru. None of them is Mumbai. Maharashtra announced in September 2025 that it would expedite a Chipi–Mumbai service and extend the runway from 2,500 m to around 3,000 m; a Navi Mumbai–Sindhudurg service was announced to start on 15 August 2026, and as at February 2026 it has not begun.

The practical gateway today is therefore Goa. Manohar International Airport at Mopa (GOX) is roughly 14 km from Sawantwadi — a sub-thirty-minute drive to parts of the corridor — with reported passenger growth of 26.6% year on year in 2024. Any honest assessment of Sindhudurg's air access should credit Mopa, not Chipi, as the thing that changed. That is also a concentration: one airport in another state, reached by one road.

The catalyst pipeline, graded by evidence

Sindhudurg marketing compresses a decade of heterogeneous announcements into one sentence: “massive infrastructure is coming”. Separated out and graded, the picture is more useful — and considerably less uniform.

The discipline this chart imposes is simple. Delivered and operating catalysts — Mopa, Chipi, the Vande Bharat, the district's NH-66 stretch, the Ro-Ro, the approved CZMP — are already capitalised into asking prices. Buying today does not capture them; it pays for them. Contracted items — the balance of NH-66, the Konkan Railway electrification package — are reasonable to underwrite with a generous slippage allowance. Announced items are a different category of claim altogether.

One thing that is frequently conflated, and should not be

Two separate road projects are routinely merged in reporting and in sales decks. The first is the four-laning of NH-66: 466 km, Panvel to Sindhudurg, under NHAI, largely built, finishing around 2026–27. The second is the greenfield Konkan Expressway: a proposed 389 km six-lane access-controlled highway by MSRDC from Chirle (Panvel) to Patradevi on the Goa border, budgeted at around ₹70,000 crore, whose revised DPR was completed in December 2024 and whose environmental clearance is still answering queries, with the alignment being reworked to avoid the Western Ghats eco-sensitive zone. These are different roads, at completely different stages. Any statement of the form “the ₹70,000 crore expressway opens in 2026” has merged them.

The calibration point

The Navi Mumbai–Chipi air service was announced with a specific date — 15 August 2026 — and that date passed without a service. It is the cheapest available lesson about this district: announcements here carry dates, and the dates slip. Apply that base rate to the Film City, the Aerocity, the runway extension and the greenfield expressway, none of which has a traceable sanctioned DPR, award or funded programme behind it. They may all happen. None of them is a schedule, and none belongs in an entry valuation as though it were.

Tourism, industry and the demand engine

What makes Sindhudurg more interesting than a generic “near Goa” story is that its demand drivers are unusually public-sector-led, which cuts both ways: state projects are slower than private ones, but they are also better documented and harder to invent.

The IHCL / Taj hotel at Shiroda–Velagar

A tripartite agreement has been signed between Indian Hotels Company Limited, the Maharashtra Tourism Development Corporation and a representative body of local landholders for a luxury hotel at Shiroda–Velagar in Vengurla taluka — a site referred to in circulating material as roughly 138 acres. The history is instructive: the land was acquired on MTDC's behalf in 1994 and the project stalled for three decades on compensation disputes, which the state has now worked to resolve. A Taj on this coast would be a genuine anchor for the premium second-home market. It is also a project whose first attempt took thirty-two years to get to an agreement.

India's first submarine and underwater museum project

The most distinctive item in the pipeline, and the furthest along. Maharashtra sanctioned a ₹110.58 crore project for passenger submarine tourism off the Sindhudurg coast near Nivati Rocks, centred on an artificial reef and underwater museum. The decommissioned Indian Navy warship ex-INS Guldar was scuttled on 19 May 2026 at a depth of about 22 metres — a physical, completed, irreversible act rather than an announcement. The programme envisages a 24-seater submarine plus guided wreck diving and tiered scuba experiences to 12, 18 and 30 metres, with public bookings expected during 2026 after safety clearances. If it runs, it gives the district something Goa does not have, which is precisely the kind of differentiated draw a tourism-dependent property market needs.

The Aerocity and the Film City

Both appear constantly in Sindhudurg marketing and both belong firmly in the Asserted column. An “Aerocity” around Chipi airport — five-star hotels, retail, offices, exhibition centres — is at concept stage. A roughly 200-acre Film City is proposed at Nandos village near the Goa border. Neither has a sanctioned detailed project report that this report could trace. The frequently repeated claim that Sindhudurg property prices have risen 25–30% “courtesy of” the Taj, the Aerocity and the Film City comes from a promotional press release issued by a firm selling plots in that market, and should be read accordingly.

Industry: the Adali pharma hub

MIDC has proposed a dedicated pharmaceutical hub at the Adali Industrial Area in Sawantwadi taluka, with internal roads, power adequate for energy-intensive pharmaceutical operations and water infrastructure reported as already laid. Kudal supports the district's other MIDC area. The strategic logic is sound — Mopa airport is about 14 km away, NH-66 and the Konkan Railway are adjacent, and the stated aim is to stop local graduates migrating to Goa for work. If it lands, it is the single most valuable development for the district's property market, because it would create the one thing Sindhudurg currently lacks: local, year-round, salaried demand rather than seasonal outside demand.

The policy tailwind

Maharashtra's Tourism Policy 2024 is materially generous to exactly this kind of project: capital subsidy of up to ₹20 crore or 20% of eligible capital investment for hospitality ventures, 15% or up to ₹15 crore for a range of tourism facilities, SGST reimbursement, electricity-duty exemption, interest subsidy of up to 5%, relaxed FSI in designated zones and fast-track approvals in 30–60 days, across 50 designated tourism hubs including Konkan beaches. A resort-led township is a plausible beneficiary. Note, though, that these incentives accrue to the developer or operator, not to a plot buyer — a distinction worth holding on to when the policy is cited in a sales conversation.

The land-value ladder

The most useful thing to understand about Sindhudurg land is that there is no single price, and the variation is not mainly geographic. It is a function of how far up the development ladder a parcel has been carried. The same acre can be quoted at ₹150 per square foot or ₹3,500 per square foot depending on whether it is an interior agricultural field, a road-fronting parcel, a converted N.A. plot, a plot inside a serviced gated layout, or a sea-view plot in a branded scheme.

The anchors this is built on

Four observations carry the chart, and all four are worth stating in their original units so they can be checked. In Kudal, residential plots about two kilometres from the railway station have been listed at ₹4 lakh per guntha — ₹367 per sq ft — while a 22-guntha parcel has been listed at ₹55 lakh, or ₹2.5 lakh per guntha (₹230 per sq ft). At Achara in Malvan taluka, sea-face land has been listed at ₹3 lakh per guntha (₹275 per sq ft). On the Mopa side around Sawantwadi, gated-layout plots are quoted at ₹7,000–9,500 per square yard (₹778–1,056 per sq ft). And across the district, plots are reported in a band of ₹1,000–2,000 per sq ft inland and ₹3,000–6,000 per sq ft for sea-view parcels.

Set the Achara figure against the sea-view band and the point becomes unmissable. Raw sea-face agricultural land at ₹275 per sq ft; developed sea-view plots at ₹3,000–6,000 per sq ft. That is an order of magnitude, and it is not created by scarcity of coastline. It is created by the work of conversion, layout sanction, servicing, amenity and branding — which is to say, it is the developer's margin plus the buyer's convenience premium.

What this means for a plot buyer

When you buy a serviced plot in a gated township you are buying two things at once: a piece of land, and a bundle of work performed on it. The land component is checkable against the ready-reckoner rate and against raw-land comparables. The work component is only worth what it delivers — and if the roads, drainage, water, power and amenities are not actually built, you have paid a developed-land price for a field. That is risk R8 in Figure 10, it is the most common failure mode in Indian plotted development, and it is why section 27 insists that amenity obligations, the maintenance corpus and the handover mechanism all appear in the agreement rather than in the brochure.

One methodological caution applies to the whole of this section. Listing asks are not transaction prices. Portal listings in thin rural markets typically sit above achieved values, sometimes substantially, and they persist unsold for long periods. The only reliable price evidence is registered transaction data from the sub-registrar for the specific village, read alongside the e-ASR ready-reckoner rate. That is task E-2 in Figure 14, and it is not optional.

The entry ticket, and the competitive set

Converting every rung of the ladder into one comparable unit — a ~600 square yard (≈5,400 sq ft) villa plot, the standard product in this market — makes the choice facing a buyer concrete.

Who else is selling this story

Sindhudurg's plotted-development market is real and already competitive, though fragmented and small-scale. The most visible branded comparable is Cida De Luxora, marketed by SSL Realty — an eleven-acre gated, Roman-themed community on the Mopa side with 600+ square yard villa plots, clubhouse and wellness landscaping, priced at ₹45–55 lakh and explicitly positioned against a stated North Goa comparable of ₹1.5–2 crore. Others market sea-touch plotting near Malvan, including a scheme described as “Ocean's Paradise” across a similar 150-acre footprint with private beach access. Alongside these sit dozens of unbranded local layouts of five to fifteen acres with no planning depth, and a substantial informal market in raw agricultural land bought directly from farmers.

Against that set, God City's differentiation is genuine: at ~150 acres it would be an order of magnitude larger than the typical branded competitor, it clears the 40-hectare Integrated Township Policy threshold that unlocks real fiscal concessions (section 16), and the Hafeez Contractor engagement — if confirmed — puts it in a different planning class entirely. Scale of that kind is not a gimmick in plotted development; it is what makes internal roads, a sewage treatment plant, a genuine clubhouse and long-horizon open space financially possible at all. Eleven acres cannot carry that infrastructure. One hundred and fifty can.

But scale cuts both ways, and this is the point on which the whole proposition turns. A 150-acre scheme is roughly fourteen times the assembly problem, fourteen times the approvals burden and — as Figure 16 sets out — something like fourteen times the capital requirement of an eleven-acre one. A small layout by a local developer with clean title is a modest, comprehensible risk. A very large layout by a two-year-old entity with no disclosed site is a much larger one. The correct conclusion is not that bigger is worse; it is that bigger raises the evidentiary bar, and at present the evidence has not been supplied.

Where this sits in India's second-home market

India's second-home market is estimated at roughly US$3.2 billion, with the retirement and second-home segment projected to reach about US$4.0 billion in 2026 on reported growth of around 23.6% a year, and luxury second-home demand growing at approximately 20% annually. The established destinations — Goa, Alibaug, Lonavala — carry average property values of roughly ₹9,860 to ₹15,000 per square foot, and Alibaug property is reported to have grown over 150% in a decade, helped latterly by the Atal Setu and its own Ro-Ro service.

Two cautions before those numbers are used. First, ₹9,860–15,000 per sq ft is built-property pricing and cannot be compared directly with Sindhudurg land rates of ₹1,000–2,000 per sq ft; comparing them is the single most common sleight of hand in coastal plot marketing. Second, those market-size and growth figures come from industry and developer sources, not from an official statistical series.

What survives the caution is the structural argument, and it is a good one. Goa is the largest and most diversified second-home market in the country and carries the highest gross rental yield potential — but it is expensive, crowded and, for non-Goan buyers, legally and politically complicated. Alibaug offers a lower entry price and superior appreciation but is tied to Mumbai weekend demand. Sindhudurg's proposition is Goa's coastline, Goa's airport and roughly a third to a tenth of Goa's land price, inside Maharashtra's legal system. For a Mumbai or Pune buyer that is a real and coherent arbitrage, and it is the reason this district deserves attention rather than dismissal.

The arbitrage has a clock on it, though, and it is worth being clear-eyed about which way that clock runs. Arbitrages of this kind close when supply arrives — and branded supply is arriving now. The window is real; it is simply not as wide as “Goa was ₹1 crore, this is ₹50 lakh, therefore it doubles twice” implies. Part IV
Law, land and regulation
The rules that decide whether a plot legally exists, whether you may buy it, whether anything may be built on it — and the one registration that converts a brochure into an enforceable contract.
15

MahaRERA — the gate everything else hangs off

The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016, administered in Maharashtra by MahaRERA, is not a formality to be cleared later. For a plot buyer it is the entire architecture of protection, and its thresholds are wide enough that essentially no township can escape them.

When registration is compulsory

Under section 3, a real estate project must be registered if the land proposed to be developed exceeds 500 square metres or the number of plots or apartments exceeds eight, inclusive of all phases. Exemption requires both limbs to be satisfied — 500 m² or less and eight units or fewer. A 150-acre scheme is roughly 607,000 square metres. There is no argument to be had.

Each phase is a separate project

This is the detail most often missed by buyers, and it is worth internalising: a registration number for Phase I says nothing whatever about the plot you are being sold in Phase II. Each phase requires its own registration, with its own timeline, its own escrow account and its own enforceable completion date. Winsten Park, as it happens, illustrates the pattern exactly — three phases, three separate UP RERA numbers, three different completion dates spanning 2020 to 2028. When a registration number is eventually produced for God City, the first question is which phase it covers and whether it covers the specific plot on offer.

What registration actually gives a buyer

  • A named promoter. A specific legal person against whom rights are enforceable — not a brand.
  • A committed completion date, from which interest for delay runs under section 18. Without registration there is no such date, and therefore no such remedy.
  • The 70% escrow rule. Seventy per cent of every receipt must go into a designated account, with withdrawals certified by the architect, the engineer and a chartered accountant. This is the single most important protection in the Act, and it does not exist before registration.
  • Disclosure. Sanctioned plans, land title documents, the area statement, the phase structure and quarterly progress updates, all published on the portal.
  • A forum. Complaints can be filed for a fee typically in the ₹1,000–5,000 range, with cases intended to be decided in about sixty days.

And what it prohibits

A project over the section 3 thresholds may not be advertised, marketed, booked or sold before it is registered. That prohibition binds the promoter, and it also exposes the agent: a channel partner working an unregistered project puts their own MahaRERA agent registration at risk. This is why Figure 19 gives brokers a different verdict from buyers.

Twenty-three of those projects are in Sindhudurg district alone. In a district with fewer than thirty towns and a modest development pipeline, twenty-three stalled registrations is not a rounding error. Execution attrition here is the norm, not the exception — which is the empirical basis for risk R17, and a good reason to check the abeyance list as well as the live register.

Who may buy land here, and what makes it buildable

Two separate legal questions are constantly conflated in plot sales, and getting them the wrong way round is expensive. The first is may I lawfully buy this? The second is may anything lawfully be built on it? A clean answer to the first tells you nothing about the second.

Section 63 — who may buy agricultural land

Under section 63 of the Maharashtra (formerly Bombay) Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act 1948, sale of agricultural land is restricted to persons who are certified agriculturists. A non-agriculturist cannot purchase farmland without the Collector's prior written permission. The 2016 amendment created important carve-outs: the bar does not apply to land within the limits of a Municipal Corporation or Municipal Council, nor to land within the jurisdiction of a Special Planning Authority or New Town Development Authority where it has been allocated to residential, commercial, industrial or other non-agricultural use. Most of rural Sindhudurg falls outside those carve-outs. A Mumbai professional buying a field near Kudal needs to know which side of that line the parcel sits on, before signing anything.

N.A. conversion — what makes land buildable

Buying lawfully does not make land buildable. Non-agricultural permission under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code is a separate process, requiring an application to the Collector with the 7/12 extract, property card, layout plan and relevant NOCs, followed by scrutiny, site inspection and payment of conversion charges. A plot sold before the N.A. order is granted is a promise, not an asset — and MahaRERA has indicated that a finalised sub-division layout, the necessary authority approvals and N.A. permission where applicable are what denote a plotted project's completion.

CRZ, the Western Ghats ESA, and the wildlife corridor

Sindhudurg is squeezed between two of India's more assertive environmental regimes. Both are capable of removing the right to build while leaving the purchase price fully paid.

The Coastal Regulation Zone

Under the CRZ Notification 2019, the coast is classified into CRZ-I (ecologically sensitive), CRZ-II (developed urban stretches), CRZ-III (rural stretches, sub-divided into IIIA and IIIB by population density) and CRZ-IV (water bodies). The Sindhudurg coast is largely CRZ-III B, which carries a No-Development Zone of 200 metres from the High Tide Line. Within that NDZ no construction is permitted, subject to narrow exceptions for repair or reconstruction of existing authorised structures within existing FSI, plinth and density, for activities specifically permitted under the notification, and for dwellings of traditional coastal communities including fisherfolk.

Three practical points. The regulator is the Maharashtra Coastal Zone Management Authority, and the Sindhudurg Coastal Zone Management Plan was approved in 2023 — so an authoritative map now exists. CRZ is not triggered only by the sea: creeks and tidal rivers draw land into the CZMP as well, which catches inland parcels that feel comfortably far from the beach. And applicability is determined survey number by survey number against the approved plan, not by eyeballing a distance on a satellite image. The line in Figure 1 is conceptual and should be treated as such.

The Western Ghats ESA and the Sawantwadi–Dodamarg corridor

On the eastern flank, the Western Ghats Ecologically Sensitive Area process continues at national level, with Maharashtra having included some 2,092 villages in draft notifications. More specifically for this district, the Bombay High Court has directed that 25 villages in Dodamarg and Sawantwadi talukas be notified as an ecologically sensitive area under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986 — with the state directed to submit a proposal to the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change and the Ministry directed to issue a final notification thereafter. The court expressly held that this corridor's designation should not wait on the broader Western Ghats notification, which is acknowledged to be a longer process. The state has separately funded a study of tiger corridors in the Dodamarg–Sawantwadi belt.

Why this is not a footnote

The Sawantwadi–Dodamarg belt is exactly the Mopa-adjacent area that Sindhudurg's plot marketing promotes most heavily — closest to the airport, closest to Goa, most in demand. An ESA notification over those villages would constrain construction, quarrying and land-use change in ways that are not yet fully defined. Any 150-acre assembly in that belt must be checked against the corridor village list, and a buyer is entitled to a written answer on it. This is risk R5, and it is scored as moderate-likelihood/high-impact for exactly this reason.

Stamp duty, the ready reckoner, and the price you actually pay

One fiscal mechanic completes the picture, because it defeats a common negotiating assumption. Stamp duty in Maharashtra is charged on the higher of the agreement value or the ready reckoner rate — never the lower. Ready reckoner rates rose by an average of about 3.89% across Maharashtra for FY 2025-26, and about 3.7% in rural areas, and the applicable rate is looked up by district, taluka, village and survey number on the state's e-ASR portal. Registration is 1% of value, capped at ₹30,000 for properties above ₹30 lakh. The practical implication: a “discount” negotiated off an inflated headline may not reduce the duty at all, and the ready-reckoner rate is also the cheapest independent sanity check available on whether a quoted price is sane. Look it up before the first meeting, not after.

What “~150 acres” actually contains

“One hundred and fifty acres” is a land area, not an inventory. Once township planning standards and Integrated Township Policy obligations are applied, roughly half of any scheme of this kind is not saleable product at all.

On this allocation, about 78 of the 150 acres are saleable plots, villas and studios. The rest is roads and circulation, statutory open space, the 15% social housing obligation, amenity and clubhouse land, utility land for the sewage and water treatment plants and the substation, and buffers for CRZ, watercourses and any ESA overlay. The buffer line is a placeholder: on a coastal parcel it can be very much larger, and it cannot be estimated at all until the survey numbers are known.

Two consequences follow. For the developer, the revenue base is roughly half the land area, and that half has to fund the servicing of the whole. For the buyer, “150 acres” conveys almost nothing about how many plots will exist, how large they will be, how much open space will survive later phases, or where the affordable component sits in the plan. Only a sanctioned layout answers those questions, which is why it appears at question three of the decision tree rather than as a nice-to-have.

The buyer's arithmetic

Whatever price is eventually published, the arithmetic below is the template to run it through. It is worked on an illustrative 5,400 sq ft (~600 sq yd) plot at ₹1,000 per sq ft — the mid-point of observed Mopa-side gated-layout rates — and every line is a real, unavoidable cost.

Three conclusions travel beyond this particular example.

The all-in cost runs roughly 20% above the headline. Stamp duty, registration, legal and survey work, development or infrastructure charges and the maintenance corpus are not optional extras. The downside is not symmetrical with the upside. The bull case requires the announced catalysts to land; the bear case requires only that they slip. And there is a tail that no smooth CAGR line captures: a plotted scheme that stalls before its amenities are built does not trade like raw land — it trades below it. A stranded plot inside a half-finished layout, with no association, no maintained roads and no water connection, is harder to sell than an open field, because the field at least has an agricultural buyer. That tail is not drawn in Figure 17, and it is the one genuinely worth insuring against. The only reliable insurance is refusing to pay before the gates in Figure 13 are cleared.

One financing constraint that shapes the whole market

Plot loans in India in 2026 typically run at 60–75% loan-to-value, tenures of 10 to 15 years and rates of roughly 8.5% to 11.5% — higher than standard home loans, because lenders price raw land as riskier. Lenders generally require the plot to be for residential use and within municipal limits, which most rural Sindhudurg layouts are not. And most Indian banks do not offer plot loans to NRIs at all. The consequence is structural rather than incidental: this is substantially a cash market, on both the buy and the sell side. That is what makes resale slow, thin and price-sensitive, and it is the mechanism behind the illiquidity risk in Figure 10. It is also the single strongest argument for buying here only with money that has no timetable attached to it.

Part VI
Risk
Eighteen exposures, ranked by likelihood and by what they cost; a capability profile measured against what a township of this scale actually demands; and a SWOT with the asymmetry left in.
23

The risk register

Three observations about the distribution, which matters more than any individual score.

The top-right quadrant is almost entirely documentary. R1 (no registration), R2 (incomplete aggregation), R3 (title defects) and R7 (capital inadequacy) are the four heaviest exposures, and every one of them is a question about paperwork and counterparty rather than about Sindhudurg. That is the most encouraging thing in the chart, because documentary risks are the kind that collapse the moment the documents appear. A MahaRERA registration and a sanctioned layout would take R1, R2, R4, R5 and R17 down sharply in a single step.

The market risks are mid-table and chronic rather than acute. R9 (exit illiquidity), R11 (announced catalysts failing to land) and R12 (the entry price already embedding delivered infrastructure) will not destroy capital outright, but they are persistent drags that a short-horizon buyer will feel and a long-horizon buyer can absorb. They argue for patience and position size rather than for avoidance.

The environmental risks are real but well down the list. R13 (monsoon, cyclone, landslide) scores as moderate on both axes. This is a wet, occasionally storm-exposed district with landslide-prone terrain inland, and that should be priced — but it is a construction and insurance question, not an existential one, and Maharashtra's Konkan Disaster Mitigation Project is actively funding coastal protection works.

The single risk most likely to be underestimated is R8 — amenities and internal infrastructure never being delivered after plots are sold. It scores 17 and it deserves it. In plotted development the developer's incentive to complete collapses once the last plot is sold, because unlike an apartment building there is no handover event that forces completion. The protection is contractual and it must be secured before payment: amenity obligations with dates, a funded maintenance corpus held independently, and a defined trigger for handover to a plot-holders' association. Section 27 puts all three in the information request.

Capability profile and SWOT

The shape is more informative than any individual score. The promoter is strong exactly where marketing is strong and weak exactly where money is at risk. Positioning, narrative and channel readiness score well; the master-planner appointment scores well and would score better with confirmation. Corporate vintage, capitalisation, regulatory evidence, entity-level delivery history, disclosure and local execution depth all score at or below one out of five.

That profile is not unusual for a genuine pre-launch venture, and it is worth saying so plainly. It is also indistinguishable, from outside, from a land-marketing exercise. The only thing that separates the two is documents — which is the argument of this entire report.

A buyer who is completely right about Sindhudurg can still lose money on this specific vehicle. The location case and the counterparty case have to be underwritten separately, and almost every sales conversation about this project is designed to let the first one carry the second.

The decision tree

Every question below is answerable from a document. None requires trusting anybody, forming a view about character, or predicting the future. Work down the left column; the moment a question cannot be answered with paper, stop at that row — the right column says what stopping should look like.

The tree is deliberately unforgiving at the top and more flexible lower down. Questions 1 to 5 are binary: either the registration, the entity match, the survey numbers, the title opinion and the CRZ and ESA position exist, or they do not, and no amount of reassurance substitutes. From question 6 onward the answers become negotiable — price, financing, amenity obligations are all things to bargain over rather than walk away from.

One instruction at the foot of the tree is worth repeating on its own, because it is the single most useful habit a plot buyer can adopt: even after every gate is cleared, tie each instalment to a verified milestone rather than to elapsed time — registration granted, N.A. order issued, layout sanctioned, plot physically demarcated on site, services actually laid. The information request — every document, in one list

This is the practical output of the whole report. Send it in writing — email, so there is a timestamp — and keep the reply, including any non-reply.   1. The MahaRERA registration number for the project, and for the specific phase containing the plot offered.

  1. The non-agricultural (N.A.) order under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code.
  2. The sanctioned sub-division layout, with the offered plot identified on it, and the approving authority's endorsement.
  3. The Integrated Township Project locational clearance, if the project is proceeding under that policy — and if so, where the 15% social housing component sits in the master plan.

  5. A written statement on Western Ghats ESA status and on whether any project village appears on the Sawantwadi–Dodamarg corridor list.

  7. Water: source, bulk allocation or sanction, and the summer yield assessment.
  8. Power sanction from MSEDCL, and the pollution-control consent for the sewage treatment plant.

D · Commercial terms

  1. The rate card, the plot schedule with areas, and the payment plan — with each instalment tied to a named, verifiable milestone.
  2. The designated (70%) account details and the bank, as recorded with MahaRERA.
  3. The draft agreement for sale, in full, with time to have it reviewed.
  4. The committed date for completion of internal infrastructure and amenities, and the consequence of missing it.
  5. The amenity schedule: what will be built, to what specification, by when, and at whose cost.
  6. The maintenance corpus — how much, who holds it, on what terms, and how it is accounted for.
  7. The handover mechanism: when the plot-holders' association is formed, what is conveyed to it, and when.
  8. All charges beyond the plot price: development charges, infrastructure charges, club membership, legal and documentation, GST where applicable.

E · Track record

  1. A list of projects completed by the contracting entity, with RERA numbers and completion certificates.
  2. For the Winsten Park credential: confirmation of the individual's role, the dates, and the current RERA status and complaint history of each phase.
  3. Written confirmation from Architect Hafeez Contractor's practice of the engagement and its scope.
Three things to do regardless of the answers

Check MahaRERA yourself. Do not accept a screenshot or a number read out over the phone. Search the portal directly — and search the lapsed and abeyance lists too, not only the live register.

Pay nothing before registration exists. Not a token, not a “pre-launch” amount, not an expression of interest, not a refundable booking. Marketing a project over the section 3 thresholds before registration is itself prohibited, and money paid at that stage sits outside the escrow mechanism entirely.

Put everything in writing and keep it. If money has already changed hands, get the receipt, the entity name and the written basis of the payment on record now — while relations are cordial — rather than later, when they may not be.

The verdict, by who you are

If the six rows have one thread running through them, it is this. Sindhudurg deserves the attention it is getting. The access story is real, the tourism pipeline is unusually well-evidenced for an Indian coastal district, the land is genuinely cheap relative to Goa, and the arbitrage a Mumbai or Pune buyer is being offered is a coherent one. Anyone dismissing the district out of hand is making a mistake.

And this specific vehicle has not yet supplied what would make it investable. Not because anything improper has been found — this report found no consumer complaint, no regulatory action, no adverse finding of any kind against God City LLP or its partners — but because almost nothing checkable has been published, and because the one thing that would change that is free to obtain and has not appeared.

Those two statements are entirely compatible. The correct posture is neither enthusiasm nor dismissal: it is patience with a checklist. Keep Sindhudurg on the list. Keep this project on a watchlist. Run G1 monthly — it takes an afternoon. And let the documents, rather than the narrative, decide when to move.

Appendix B

Glossary of Maharashtra land and planning terms

TermMeaning, and why it matters here
7/12 extract (Satbara)The core land record for rural Maharashtra, combining the record of rights (Form VII) with the crop register (Form XII). Shows the occupant, the area, the tenure class and encumbrances. The first document to ask for, and the one indexed by survey number.
8-A extractThe account of a holder's total land in a village. Used alongside the 7/12 to confirm holdings and to cross-check ceiling and tenancy positions.
Gat number / survey numberThe cadastral identifier for a parcel. Nothing — no title search, no CRZ check, no ESA check, no valuation — can be done without it. Its absence is the reason Figure 13 stops at question three.
Itar HakkThe "other rights" column on the 7/12. Records tenancy, mortgages, government interest, grant conditions, court orders and any requirement for the Collector's permission to transfer. A blank column is good news; an entry is a stop sign.
Kul / Kul Kayda / section 32GTenancy law giving long-term tenant farmers security of tenure and a statutory route to ownership. A 32G entry records that process. Deleted tenant entries without a proper certificate warrant close scrutiny.
Inam / Watan / DevasthanHistoric grant tenures. Devasthan land is held for a religious institution and is frequently inalienable; where transfer is possible it needs government permission and typically Charity Commissioner approval.
Occupant Class-I / Class-IIClass-I is unrestricted tenure. Class-II ("new tenure") is restricted — granted or converted subject to conditions, requiring prior sanction and usually a premium before resale. A sale without sanction can be void.
N.A. (non-agricultural) orderPermission under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code converting land from agricultural to a specified non-agricultural use. Buying land lawfully does not make it buildable; this does.
Ready reckoner rate (e-ASR)The government's notified minimum value for a property, by district, taluka, village and survey number. Stamp duty is charged on the higher of this or the agreement value, and it is the cheapest independent sanity check on a quoted price.
GunthaLocal land unit. 1 guntha = 1,089 sq ft ≈ 101.2 m²; 40 guntha = 1 acre. Konkan land is quoted per guntha far more often than per square foot.
CRZ / CZMP / NDZ / HTLCoastal Regulation Zone; the Coastal Zone Management Plan that maps it (Sindhudurg's approved in 2023); the No-Development Zone within it (200 m in CRZ-III B); measured from the High Tide Line. Creeks and tidal rivers draw inland land into the plan too.
ESA / ESZEcologically Sensitive Area or Zone, notified under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986. Relevant here through the Western Ghats process and the directed Sawantwadi–Dodamarg 25-village corridor.
ITPIntegrated Township Project under Maharashtra's township policy. Minimum 40 ha contiguous, all parcels owned by the proponent, ≥15% of residential FSI for social housing, in exchange for stamp-duty and development-charge concessions.
FSIFloor Space Index — the ratio of permissible built-up area to plot area. The social housing obligation under the ITP is expressed as a share of residential FSI, not of land.
Designated account (70% escrow)The account into which 70% of a registered project's receipts must be deposited, with withdrawals certified by architect, engineer and chartered accountant. It exists only after registration.
Abeyance / lapsed projectMahaRERA's status for registrations that have expired without completion or extension. Twenty-three Sindhudurg projects currently sit in this category — a list worth checking alongside the live register.
CAM / maintenance corpusCommon area maintenance, and the fund meant to pay for it after handover. In plotted development this is where schemes most often fail quietly, years after the last plot is sold.

Appendix D

Method, scope and limitations

What was done

Systematic public-domain research, conducted in February 2026, covering: the promoter's own published material; corporate registry data; MahaRERA and UP RERA registers; the statutory and policy instruments listed in Appendix C; district and Census data; infrastructure and tourism project reporting; listing-portal price data for Sindhudurg and for the Greater Noida comparable; and secondary market commentary. Findings were assembled into the nineteen figures, each of which carries its own source line, and into the fact register at Appendix A.

What was not done, and it matters

  • No site visit. No parcel has been identified, so none was possible. Even if one had been, a single dry-season visit would not answer the drainage and water questions raised in section 08.
  • No interviews. The promoter, the architect's practice, VHR Group, SKV Infotech, MahaRERA officials, local advocates and Sindhudurg brokers were not approached. Several of the open questions in this report would be answered quickly by a conversation, and section 27 is written to make those conversations productive.
  • No document inspection. No 7/12 extract, title deed, layout, approval or agreement was examined, because none is public.
  • No physical search of registers. Sub-registrar transaction data, the e-ASR rate for any specific village, and the Collector's records were not physically searched. Doing so requires survey numbers.
  • Portal access was partly indirect. Some primary portals could not be retrieved directly in this research environment; where a claim rests on secondary reporting of a primary record, it is graded Reported rather than Register, and readers should verify it at source before relying on it.

Known weaknesses in the evidence base

Listing asks are not transactions. Every price in Figures 5 and 6 that is anchored to a listing is an asking price. In thin rural markets these can sit materially above achieved values and can persist unsold for long periods. The ready-reckoner rate and registered comparables are the corrective, and both require survey numbers.

Several market statistics originate with interested parties. The "+28% within 25–30 km of Mopa", the "25–30% district-wide", the "20–30% highway frontage premium" and the North Goa comparison all come from firms selling plots in this market. They are reported here because they form part of the information environment, they are labelled every time, and no conclusion rests on them.

Two constructed models carry assumptions, not facts. The ₹389 crore capital build-up in Figure 16 and the land budget in Figure 15 are this report's own estimates from stated assumptions. They are intended to establish magnitude — a township of this scale needs hundreds of crores, and roughly half of any such site is not saleable — and they should not be quoted as findings about this project's actual cost plan or layout.

Scores are judgements. The capability profile in Figure 9 and the risk register in Figure 10 are this report's assessments, shown with their reasoning precisely so that a reader who disagrees can substitute their own and see what changes.

Three things this report explicitly does not claim

It does not claim the project will fail. A well-capitalised, well-advised township could be built on exactly this thesis, and the master-planner appointment suggests that may be the intent.

It does not allege wrongdoing. No complaint, regulatory action or adverse finding was located against God City LLP or its partners. A null result is not a clearance, but neither is it an accusation, and it should not be read as one.

And it does not claim the documents do not exist. "Not found in the public domain" means a search was run and returned nothing. A promoter may hold every document listed in section 27 and simply not publish it. The purpose of this report is to identify precisely which documents would resolve the uncertainty — and to make asking for them straightforward.
