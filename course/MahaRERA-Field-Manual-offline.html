Three things to know before you begin

1. RERA is not MahaRERA. RERA is the central Act of Parliament (Act 16 of 2016). MahaRERA is the authority constituted for Maharashtra under it. The Act is the same across India; the rules, fees, forms, orders and circulars are Maharashtra-specific. There is no such thing as "the MahaRERA Act" — writing that in the exam costs you the mark. This course keeps them separate everywhere.

Module 1 — Real Estate Orientation

1. What I need to learn

By the end of this module you should be able to walk into a room, hear someone say "the promoter is doing a 33(7) with a PMC on board, we're pre-launch, RERA's applied for" and understand every word of it.

Three things: what real estate is, who the players are, and what order things happen in.

2. Simple explanation

2.1 What is real estate?

Real estate is land plus everything permanently attached to it, plus the bundle of legal rights that come with it. That second half matters more than the first. In India you almost never buy "a flat" — you buy a set of rights: the right to occupy, to use, to transfer, to mortgage, to exclude others. A flat with a disputed title is bricks you cannot sell.

An agent who thinks of property as bricks gives bad advice. An agent who thinks of property as a rights package with a paper trail gives good advice. That single mental shift is the point of this whole course.

2.3 Market types

Primary market — buying from the promoter. First sale. GST may apply, RERA project registration applies, Agreement for Sale is the instrument.

Secondary market (resale) — buying from an existing allottee/owner. No GST. RERA project registration may have lapsed or completed. Instrument is usually a Sale Deed or a transfer of the earlier agreement. Title chain matters far more here because you inherit every past defect.

8. Case study 📋

The two-brand problem.

A buyer tells you: "I'm buying a Lodha flat in Thane." You look it up and the promoter on the MahaRERA registration is a special purpose vehicle with a completely different name, and the land is owned by a third entity that entered a JDA.

Questions to sit with:

  • Who does the buyer have a contract with?
  • If the project is delayed 2 years, who does the buyer file against under s.31?
  • Does the parent brand's balance sheet protect the buyer?

The point: in Indian real estate the brand you see is rarely the legal entity you contract with. Projects are routinely built in SPVs. This is normal and lawful — but it means brand reputation is not the same as counterparty strength, and your client should know the difference. Everything in Module 8 (Due Diligence) exists because of this gap.

Answers
  1. Yes — s.2(zk) is deliberately wide and can make the landowner a co-promoter, jointly liable.
  2. Broker/agent is a legal category under s.2(zm) requiring registration. Channel partner is an industry arrangement (empanelment with a developer) and carries no separate legal status — a channel partner still needs agent registration.

  4. Architect (Form 1), Structural Engineer (Form 2), Chartered Accountant (Form 3).

  6. 10% — s.13(1).
  7. Corporate leasing demand, rent per sq ft / cap rates, grade and floor-plate efficiency, tenant credit quality (any three).
  8. No — GST applies only to under-construction sale by a promoter; resale of a completed unit is a transfer of immovable property and outside GST.
  9. Because in resale you inherit the entire prior chain of title; any defect anywhere in that chain travels to your buyer.
  10. Projects are commonly built in SPVs or joint ventures for finance and risk-ring-fencing reasons.

12. One-page revision sheet

Real estate = land + attachments + a bundle of legal rights. The rights are the asset.

Promoter (s.2zk) is wider than developer — includes JDA landowners. Agent (s.2zm) = negotiates for a fee. Allottee (s.2d) = buyer, incl. subsequent purchaser, excl. tenant.

Lifecycle: Land → Planning → Approvals → Development → Construction → Marketing → Sales → Agreement → Registration → Possession.

Two hard rules to never forget: ⚖️ s.3 — no advertising/marketing/booking/selling before project registration. ⚖️ s.13(1) — no more than 10% before a registered Agreement for Sale.

Three certificates: IOD (permission to build to a stage) → CC (start) → OC (occupy). Three forms: 1 Architect · 2 Engineer · 3 CA.

Brand ≠ legal entity. Always check who the promoter actually is.

Module 2 — The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016

Official syllabus: MATP Module 2 — 2.1 Preamble, Purpose and Objectives · 2.2 Sections of the Act · 2.3 Key Components. Exam weight: 🔴 Very high. This and Module 5 carry the most marks. Time: 6–8 hours. Do not rush this module.

1. What I need to learn

Not the whole Act. Roughly 25 sections carry almost all the exam value and all the day-to-day agent value. This module gives you those, in the order that makes them make sense.

The mental model: the Act does four things.

  1. Forces disclosure (register the project, publish the details).
  2. Controls the money (70% in a separate account, 10% cap before agreement).
  3. Fixes accountability (promoter duties, agent duties, allottee duties).
  4. Gives a fast remedy (a regulator and a tribunal instead of civil courts).

Everything else is machinery.

2. Simple explanation

2.1 Pre-RERA: why this law exists

Before 2016, an Indian homebuyer's position was roughly this:

  • The builder wrote the agreement. It was one-sided, often unregistered, and the buyer signed it because the alternative was not buying.
  • Area was sold in "super built-up" — a number the builder defined. Two flats sold as "1,000 sq ft" could differ by 200 sq ft of actual usable space.
  • Money from Project A funded land for Project B. When B failed, A stalled. Buyers paying EMIs on a flat that did not exist.
  • Delay had no price. The agreement gave the builder 6 months' grace, then 12 more, then "force majeure".
  • The remedy was a civil suit or a consumer forum. Both took years.
  • Brokers were entirely unregulated.

RERA is a direct answer to each of those. When you're studying a section, ask "which pre-RERA abuse is this killing?" — that's usually the fastest way to remember it.

2.2 Preamble, purpose and objectives (syllabus 2.1)

The Act's own preamble says it is to establish the Authority for regulation and promotion of the sector, to ensure sale of plot/apartment/building in an efficient and transparent manner, to protect the interest of consumers, to establish an adjudicating mechanism for speedy dispute redressal, and to establish an Appellate Tribunal.

Note the ordering — regulation and promotion. RERA is not purely a consumer statute; it is also meant to bring institutional capital into a sector that was too opaque to invest in.

2.4 Key components (syllabus 2.3) — the six that matter

① Project registration (s.3). No advertising, marketing, booking, selling or offering for sale without registration, in any planning area, for projects above the threshold. The threshold (s.3(2)(a)): land over 500 sq m or more than 8 apartments, inclusive of all phases. Also exempt: projects where completion certificate was obtained before commencement of the Act, and renovation/repair not involving marketing or new allotment. 👉 Each phase is a standalone project and registers separately.

② The 70% rule (s.4(2)(l)(D)). Seventy per cent of amounts realised from allottees goes into a separate account in a scheduled bank, to cover land and construction cost only, withdrawn in proportion to completion and certified by Architect, Engineer and CA. This is the single provision that killed cross-project fund diversion.

③ The 10% rule (s.13(1)). A promoter shall not accept more than 10% of the cost of the apartment/plot/building as an advance or application fee without first entering into a written Agreement for Sale and registering it.

④ Carpet area (s.2(k)). The net usable floor area of an apartment, excluding external walls, service shafts, exclusive balcony/verandah and exclusive open terrace, but including internal partition walls. Sale must be on carpet area. This ended super-built-up selling.

⑤ Interest is symmetrical (s.18, s.19(7)). If the promoter delays, they pay interest to the allottee; if the allottee delays payment, they pay interest to the promoter — at the same rate prescribed by state rules. This symmetry is a favourite exam point.

⑥ Defect liability — 5 years (s.14(3)). Any structural defect or defect in workmanship, quality or provision of services brought to the promoter's notice within 5 years of possession must be rectified within 30 days without further charge; failing which, the allottee is entitled to compensation.

8. Case study 📋

The pre-launch that wasn't.

A developer offers you 2% brokerage — double the market rate — to bring buyers into a "pre-launch" at ₹18,500/sq ft, against a post-launch price of ₹21,000. Booking amount is ₹5 lakh on a ₹2.4 crore flat. The developer says RERA registration is "filed, number expected in three weeks." There is an allotment letter, no Agreement for Sale.

Work through it:

  1. Is marketing this project lawful right now?
  2. Which sections are being contravened, and by whom?
  3. Is the ₹5 lakh booking within or outside the 10% rule?
  4. What is your exposure if you introduce a buyer?
  5. What would you tell the buyer if they insist the discount is worth it?
Analysis
  1. No. s.3 bars advertising, marketing, booking, selling or offering for sale before registration. "Filed" is not "registered". s.5 gives the Authority 30 days, and deemed registration only arises if the Authority fails to act — you cannot market on the assumption.
  2. The promoter contravenes s.3 (penalty under s.59 — up to 10% of estimated project cost). You contravene s.10(a) the moment you facilitate a sale in an unregistered project.
  3. ₹5 lakh on ₹2.4 crore is ~2.08% — within the 10% cap, so s.13 is not breached by amount. But s.13 is not the live problem here; s.3 is. A booking in an unregistered project is unlawful regardless of how small the cheque is.
  4. s.62: ₹10,000 per day for as long as the default continues, cumulatively up to **5% of the cost of the apartment** — on a ₹2.4 crore flat that ceiling is ₹12 lakh. Plus your registration is at risk. The 1% extra brokerage is ₹2.4 lakh. The maths is not close.
  5. Tell them plainly: until the registration number exists, they have **no registered project, no model agreement, no separate bank account protection and no MahaRERA forum** if it goes wrong. The discount is compensation for exactly that risk. Advise waiting for the number — and if the developer's pricing is real, it will still be there.

The professional point: "pre-launch" is the most common way agents lose their registration. The economics always look good and the legal position is never defensible.

1. What I need to learn

2. Simple explanation

2.1 RERA vs MahaRERA — get this right permanently

RERAMahaRERA
What it isA central Act of Parliament — Act 16 of 2016The regulatory authority for Maharashtra, constituted under that Act
Made byParliament of IndiaGovernment of Maharashtra (s.20)
AppliesAll IndiaMaharashtra only
Changes howAmendment by ParliamentOrders, circulars, regulations by the Authority; Rules by the State Government
You'd cite it as"Section 13 of the Act""MahaRERA Order No. 46C/2025"

Say it once, correctly: RERA is the law. MahaRERA is the regulator that enforces the law in Maharashtra. There is no such thing as "the MahaRERA Act". If you write that in the exam, you lose the mark.

The three-layer stack you are actually governed by:

LAYER 1  RERA Act, 2016 (Parliament)              ── the principles
LAYER 2  Maharashtra RERA Rules, 2017 (State Govt) ── the numbers: fees, interest, forms
LAYER 3  MahaRERA Regulations / Orders / Circulars ── the current operating rules

Layer 3 changes several times a year. This is the layer where agents get caught out, because they learned the rules once in 2019 and never re-read.

2.2 Key pillars of MahaRERA (syllabus 3.1)

Pillar 1 — Registration. Projects (s.3–5) and agents (s.9). Everything begins here: nothing gets a legal existence in Maharashtra real estate marketing until it has a MahaRERA number.

Pillar 2 — Disclosure. A public, searchable web database (s.34(b)). Project details, promoter details, approvals, sanctioned plans, quarterly progress, litigation. This is the pillar you'll use every working day — Module 4 is entirely about it.

Pillar 3 — Compliance monitoring. Quarterly Progress Reports, the separate-account regime, Forms 1/2/3, advertising norms, agent certification. MahaRERA has moved steadily from a registration body to a monitoring body.

Pillar 4 — Dispute resolution. Three routes with different characters: Conciliation (voluntary, fast, no adjudication) → Complaint to the Authority (s.31) → Adjudicating Officer (s.71, compensation only) → Appeal to the Appellate Tribunal (s.43, 60 days) → High Court (s.58, question of law).

Pillar 5 — Enforcement. Orders, penalties, recovery as arrears of land revenue (s.40), project de-registration, and suspension/revocation of agent registration.

2.3 Powers and functions of the Authority

Drawn from ss.34–38:

  • s.34 — register and regulate projects and agents; maintain the website database; ensure compliance.
  • s.35 — call for information and conduct investigations; powers of a civil court in respect of summoning, discovery, evidence.
  • s.36 — issue interim orders during an inquiry.
  • s.37 — issue directions binding on promoters, agents and allottees.
  • s.38 — impose penalty or interest; regulate its own procedure.
  • s.40 — recover dues as arrears of land revenue.

The practical meaning for you: MahaRERA can summon you, can order you, and can recover money from you through the revenue machinery. It is not a grievance desk.

2.4 How MahaRERA regulates agents specifically

Four levers, all of which are examinable:

  1. Registration (s.9 + Maharashtra Rules) — mandatory, 5-year validity.
  2. Competency certification — MahaRERA made training + a certification exam a condition of new registration and renewal. This is the reason your MATP course exists.
  3. Advertising discipline — the registration number, website address and QR code must appear in agents' advertisements to prescribed standards.
  4. Penalty and revocation — s.62, s.65, s.66, plus suspension/revocation of registration.

2.5 How MahaRERA regulates promoters

Registration and disclosure under ss.3–5; QPR filing on a financial-quarter cycle; the three-bank-account structure; model allotment letter and agreement for sale proformas; advertising norms; and de-registration of stalled projects.

8. Case study 📋

The stale advertisement.

You've been running a hoarding and an Instagram campaign for a Thane project for eight months. The creative shows the project name, your phone number in large type, and the MahaRERA project registration number in small text at the bottom. There is no QR code and your own agent registration number does not appear.

Questions:

  1. Which order governs this, and what does it require?
  2. What is the exposure per violation?
  3. Is "the developer designed the creative" a defence?
  4. What do you change, today?
Analysis
  1. Order No. 46C/2025 (8 April 2025). It requires that the MahaRERA registration number and the website address be displayed in a font equal to or larger than the largest font used for contact details/address, that the registration number, website address and QR code be placed in the top-right corner, and that the QR code be scannable with correct aspect ratio.
  2. ₹10,000 to ₹50,000 per violation, and failure to correct within 10 days is treated as a continuing offence with further action under the Act.
  3. No. The order binds promoters and registered real estate agents. If the advertisement is yours — your number, your leads — it is your compliance obligation. Practically: never run a developer's creative without checking it against the order yourself.
  4. Rebuild the creative: QR + registration number + website in the top-right, font sized against your own contact details, your agent registration number displayed, and re-check every live asset — hoardings, portals, social, WhatsApp forwards, brochures.

The point: your phone number being the biggest thing on the ad is exactly what the order targets. Advertising compliance is the most common and most easily avoided agent penalty.

3. 🔴 The 30-Minute MahaRERA Project Investigation

Work these fifteen steps in order. Keep a notepad open. By step 15 you'll have a defensible view.

Step 1 — Search the project (2 min)

Where to click → Public site → search registered projects. Search by project name, MahaRERA registration number, or promoter name. If you have a hoarding, scan the QR code. What to look for → An exact match, and the correct one. Common project names repeat across the state. What good looks like → One clear record, with a registration number in the standard format, in the district and taluka you expected. 🚩 Red flag → No record at all; or several near-identical names by different promoters; or the project is only findable under a name the salesperson never mentioned. Ask the developer → "Give me the exact MahaRERA registration number and the registered project name."

Step 2 — Verify registration status and validity (2 min)

Where to click → The project record header. What to look for → Registration number, status (registered / lapsed / de-registered / extended), and the proposed completion date. What good looks like → Status active, completion date in the future with realistic headroom. 🚩 Red flag → Lapsed or de-registered; or a completion date already past. A lapsed registration means the promoter may not legally market the project. Ask → "Is the registration currently valid, and has it ever lapsed or been extended?"

Step 3 — Check the promoter (3 min)

Where to click → Promoter details tab. What to look for → Legal entity name, type (individual / partnership / LLP / company), PAN-level identity, address, and the list of the promoter's other registered projects. What good looks like → A promoter with several past projects that reached completion, and whose brand name and legal name are reconcilable. 🚩 Red flag → Brand-new SPV with no track record; or a promoter whose other projects show lapsed registrations, repeated extensions or a pile of complaints. Ask → "Which entity is the promoter, and can you list your last five completed projects with their registration numbers?"

This step is why Module 1 insisted brand ≠ legal entity. Use the promoter search to see the portfolio, not the project in isolation.

Step 4 — Check project status and progress (3 min)

Where to click → Project details → status / progress section. What to look for → Declared percentage completion, work stage (excavation, plinth, slabs, finishing), and consistency with the completion date. What good looks like → Progress consistent with the timeline and with what you can see on site. 🚩 Red flag → Progress flat across consecutive quarters; or a project 30% complete with 9 months to the declared completion date. Ask → "Show me the last four quarterly progress filings."

Step 5 — Check the completion date and any extensions (2 min)

Where to click → Registration details; extension records. What to look for → Original proposed completion date vs current date, and every extension. What good looks like → Original date intact, or a single well-documented extension. 🚩 Red flag → Multiple extensions. Under s.6 an extension is ordinarily up to one year and for force majeure. A pattern of extensions is a pattern of missed promises. Ask → "What was the original completion date, how many extensions have you taken, and on what grounds?"

Step 6 — Check land details (3 min)

Where to click → Land details / project details tab. What to look for → Survey number / CTS number, total plot area, and the nature of the promoter's right — ownership, development agreement, lease. What good looks like → Land area matching the sanctioned plan, clean survey identification. 🚩 Red flag → The promoter holds only development rights and the landowner is not a co-promoter; area mismatches between land details and plans. Ask → "Who owns the land, and if it's a JDA, is the landowner a co-promoter on the registration?" Cross-check → Take the survey/CTS number to IGR Maharashtra for the property card and the registered land document.

Step 7 — Check approvals (3 min)

Where to click → Approvals / documents section. What to look for → IOD, Commencement Certificate, layout approval, and NOCs (fire, environment, aviation, water, traffic) — with dates and issuing authority. What good looks like → CC issued and covering the floors actually being sold. 🚩 Red flag → CC absent or covering fewer floors than the tower being sold; approvals described as "applied for"; NOCs missing on a high-rise. Ask → "Give me the CC and tell me up to which floor it's valid." Cross-check → MCGM AutoDCR for Greater Mumbai proposals.

Step 8 — Check sanctioned plans (2 min)

Where to click → Uploaded plans/documents. What to look for → Sanctioned floor plans and layout as approved. What good looks like → Sanctioned plan matches the brochure — same tower count, same floors, same unit mix. 🚩 Red flag → Brochure shows amenities or a tower the sanctioned plan does not. Under s.14 the promoter must build per the sanctioned plan; alterations beyond minor additions need 2/3rds allottee consent. Ask → "Show me the sanctioned plan for this tower and this floor."

Step 9 — Check building and unit details (2 min)

Where to click → Building details / apartment details. What to look for → Number of buildings, floors, unit mix, and total units — declared vs marketed. What good looks like → The flat being sold to your client exists, on the declared floor, in the declared tower. 🚩 Red flag → More units marketed than registered; or a "new tower" that isn't on the registration. Ask → "Is this unit within the registered inventory? Show me it in the disclosure."

Step 10 — Check carpet area (2 min)

Where to click → Apartment details — declared carpet area. What to look for → The RERA carpet area for the specific unit type, in sq m and sq ft. What good looks like → The carpet area in the disclosure matches the number in the cost sheet and the draft agreement. 🚩 Red flag → Salesperson quoting "saleable" or "super built-up"; carpet in the agreement lower than in the disclosure. ⚖️ Sale must be on carpet area (s.2(k)) — see Module 10 for the maths. Ask → "Confirm the RERA carpet area in writing, and show me the same number in the draft agreement."

Step 11 — Check quarterly updates (QPR) (3 min)

Where to click → Quarterly progress / project updates. What to look for → Filings for each of the last four quarters, physical and financial progress. Financial-quarter cycle: due by the 20th of July, October, January and April. What good looks like → Four consecutive filings, on time, showing real movement. 🚩 Red flag → Missing quarters, or filings that repeat identical percentages. A promoter who won't file a form on time is telling you something about how they run a site. Ask → "Why is Q__ missing?"

Step 12 — Check litigation and complaints (3 min)

Where to click → Project's litigation disclosure and the complaints/orders database at Orders & Judgments. What to look for → Pending cases disclosed by the promoter, plus complaints filed against this project or this promoter and how they were decided. What good looks like → No litigation, or minor disclosed matters unrelated to title or delivery. 🚩 Red flag → Title litigation; multiple s.18 delay complaints; adverse orders the promoter hasn't complied with. Ask → "List every pending case on this land and this project, and every MahaRERA complaint against you."

Step 13 — Check encumbrances (2 min)

Where to click → Portal's encumbrance disclosure, then cross-check at IGR. What to look for → Mortgages, charges, or lender security on the land or the project. What good looks like → Either no encumbrance, or a construction-finance mortgage the lender will release unit-by-unit (normal and disclosed). 🚩 Red flag → Undisclosed mortgage; or a lender charge with no NOC process explained. Ask → "Is the land mortgaged? Which lender, and what's the NOC process for my buyer's flat?" Cross-check → IGR Maharashtra search / encumbrance certificate.

Step 14 — Check possession position (2 min)

Where to click → Completion status; OC where uploaded. What to look for → Whether Occupancy Certificate has been obtained (full or part), and the declared possession timeline. What good looks like → For a ready flat, a full OC covering the specific wing and floor. 🚩 Red flag → Possession being offered with no OC, or only a part-OC that doesn't cover the unit. Never let a client take possession of a unit not covered by an OC. Ask → "Show me the OC and confirm it covers this wing and this floor."

Step 15 — Check remaining disclosures (2 min)

Where to click → The rest of the project record. What to look for → Proforma allotment letter, agreement for sale and conveyance deed (s.4(2)(g)); parking disclosure; common areas and amenities; the bank account details; professional team (architect, engineer, CA); any deviation sheet against the model agreement. What good looks like → All three proformas uploaded; the agreement is the Annexure A model form or has a clear deviation sheet with modifications highlighted. 🚩 Red flag → No proforma agreement uploaded; deviations from the model form that are not highlighted; amenities in the brochure absent from the disclosure. Ask → "Send me the uploaded proforma agreement and the deviation sheet."

Closing the loop

After 15 steps, write five lines: what the project is, who the promoter is, the biggest risk, the one document you still need, and whether you would put your own client into it.

That five-line note is your product. It is what a client is actually paying you for.

7. Case study 📋

Two towers, one registration.

You investigate a Thane project. Steps 1–5 look clean: registered, active, completion date 18 months out. At Step 7 the CC covers up to the 22nd floor. At Step 9 the disclosure lists two towers, A and B. The brochure shows three towers and your client is being sold a 34th-floor flat in Tower C.

Questions: What has gone wrong? Which sections bite? What do you tell the client?

Analysis

Two separate problems, and both are serious.

Problem 1 — Tower C. If Tower C isn't in the registration, then either it is a separate phase that must be separately registered (each phase is a standalone project under the Act), or it isn't approved at all. Marketing it engages s.3, and your facilitating it engages s.10(a) with penalty under s.62.

Problem 2 — the 34th floor. The CC covers 22 floors. Floors above a CC's coverage are not sanctioned for construction. Selling a 34th-floor flat against a 22-floor CC means selling something that has no present legal permission to exist. Developers do routinely obtain CC in stages and extend it — that is normal — but "we'll get it later" is a risk your buyer is carrying, not a technicality.

What you tell the client: don't book. Ask for (a) the registration number for Tower C, and (b) the amended CC covering floor 34. If both arrive, re-run steps 7–9 and reconsider. If the developer treats the questions as obstruction, that is itself the answer.

Why this case is here: it is built from the two most common real gaps between a brochure and a portal record. Steps 7, 8 and 9 exist specifically to catch them.

1. What I need to learn

Your legal identity, how you acquire it, how you keep it, how you lose it, and what you must do every day to stay on the right side of it.

2. Simple explanation

2.1 Who is a real estate agent? (syllabus 5.1) ⚖️

Section 2(zm) — a real estate agent is a person who negotiates or acts on behalf of one person in a transaction of transfer of a plot, apartment or building in a real estate project, and receives remuneration, fees or any other charge for their services. It expressly includes property dealers, brokers and middlemen by whatever name called.

Two tests, both must be present:

  1. You negotiate or act for another person in a transfer, and
  2. You receive consideration for it.

Therefore:

  • Introducing a buyer to a developer for a commission → agent.
  • Being a salaried employee of the promoter selling its own project → not an agent (you're the promoter's staff).
  • "Channel partner", "sourcing partner", "associate", "consultant" → agent, whatever the contract calls it. The Act says by whatever name called.
  • Doing one deal as a favour, with a "gift" afterwards → treat as agent. Don't test this.

2.2 Registration under Section 9 (syllabus 5.2) ⚖️

s.9(1): No real estate agent shall facilitate the sale or purchase of, or act on behalf of any person in, any plot/apartment/building in a real estate project registered under this Act without obtaining registration.

Note the precision: the obligation attaches to dealing in a registered project. Read together with s.10(a) — which forbids facilitating a transaction in a project that required registration but doesn't have it — the combined effect is simple and total: you need to be registered, and you may only deal in projects that are registered.

s.9(6): registration may be revoked or suspended after an opportunity of being heard.

2.7 Functions and duties of agents (syllabus 5.6) ⚖️ — Section 10

A registered agent shall not:

  • (a) facilitate the sale/purchase of any plot/apartment/building in a project not registered under the Act where registration is required;
  • (c) involve themselves in any unfair trade practice, including:
    • making any statement, orally or in writing, which misrepresents the services, standard, quality or grade of the project;
    • representing that the promoter or agent has approvals or affiliations they do not have;
    • making a false or misleading representation about the services;
    • permitting the publication of an advertisement of services that are not intended to be offered.

A registered agent shall:

  • (b) maintain and preserve books of account, records and documents as prescribed;
  • (d) facilitate the possession of all information and documents to which the allottee is entitled at the time of booking;
  • (e) discharge such other functions as may be prescribed.

2.8 Revocation (syllabus 5.7)

Under s.9(6), registration may be revoked or suspended where the agent:

  • is in breach of the conditions of registration;
  • makes a wilful default or contravenes the Act, rules or regulations;
  • is found to have obtained registration through misrepresentation or fraud.

An opportunity of being heard must be given.

8. Case study 📋

The friendly referral.

A former client asks you to help their cousin buy a resale flat in a 2012 building — completed long before RERA, never registered. You're not advertising, not representing the developer; you just introduce buyer to seller and take ₹2 lakh.

Separately, the same month, you introduce a buyer to a pre-launch in an unregistered under-construction project and take ₹3 lakh.

Question: are you in breach in either case? Both? Neither?

Analysis

Case 1 — the 2012 resale: not a s.10(a) breach. s.10(a) prohibits facilitating a sale in a project that is required to be registered but isn't. A building completed before the Act commenced, with its completion certificate obtained pre-commencement, falls outside the registration requirement. There is no unregistered project being marketed here.

But note carefully: s.9 still describes you as a real estate agent, and you are taking a fee for negotiating a transfer. The prudent position — and the one MahaRERA's own guidance points to — is that an agent operating in Maharashtra registers and stays registered, and confines the question to which projects they may deal in. Doing resale work without registration while holding yourself out as an agent is not a position worth defending.

Case 2 — the pre-launch: a clear breach of s.10(a). The project requires registration and doesn't have it. Penalty under s.62: ₹10,000 per day of continuing default, up to 5% of the cost of the apartment. On a ₹2.5 crore flat that's a ceiling of ₹12.5 lakh against your ₹3 lakh fee. Your registration is also exposed under s.9(6).

The distinction to carry away: RERA regulates projects requiring registration. Your personal registration is the baseline; the project's registration determines whether you may deal in it at all. Check both, every time — and when in doubt about an old building, check whether a completion certificate pre-dates the Act.

The promoter's obligations — because every promoter duty is an agent opportunity. Each thing the promoter must disclose is something you get to inspect; each thing they must do is something you can check they've done.

2. Simple explanation

2.1 Registration of real estate projects (syllabus 6.1) ⚖️ s.3

No promoter shall advertise, market, book, sell or offer for sale, or invite persons to purchase any plot, apartment or building in any planning area without registering the project.

Exemptions — s.3(2):

ExemptionDetail
(a) Small projectsLand not exceeding 500 sq m or apartments not exceeding 8, inclusive of all phases. State Government may reduce these thresholds.
(b) Completed projectsCompletion certificate received before commencement of the Act.
(c) Renovation/repair/redevelopmentWhere it does not involve marketing, advertising, selling or new allotment.

Two things people get wrong:

  • The threshold is "or", not "and" — a 400 sq m plot with 12 apartments still needs registration.
  • Each phase is a separate project and registers separately. "Phase 2 is covered by Phase 1's number" is wrong.

2.2 Registration application & process (syllabus 6.2) ⚖️ s.4

The promoter applies to the Authority with prescribed fee and documents. s.4(2) requires:

ClauseDisclosure
(a)Promoter details — name, address, type of enterprise, registered particulars
(b)Track record — projects launched in the last 5 years, status, delays, pending cases
(c)Authenticated copy of approvals and commencement certificate
(d)Sanctioned plan, layout plan, specifications
(e)Plan of development works, proposed facilities, fire-fighting, drinking water, emergency management
(f)Location details with clear demarcation of land
(g)Proforma of the allotment letter, agreement for sale and conveyance deed
(h)Number, type and carpet area of apartments; number and areas of garages
(i)Names and addresses of real estate agents, contractors, architect, structural engineer
(j)Declaration of legal title with authenticated documents
(k)Details of encumbrances, including litigation, and any rights/title/interest/name of a party in possession
(l)Declaration supported by affidavit — including the 70% separate account, timelines, and no false statement

s.4(2)(l)(D) — the 70% rule. Seventy per cent of amounts realised from allottees goes into a separate account in a scheduled bank, used only to cover land cost and construction cost, withdrawn in proportion to completion, certified by Architect, Engineer and CA.

Grant of registration — s.5. The Authority grants or rejects within 30 days. If it neither grants nor rejects, the project is deemed registered and the Authority provides the registration number. Rejection requires an opportunity of being heard.

2.3 ⏱ The three-bank-account structure (current Maharashtra practice)

MahaRERA operationalised the 70% rule into a three-account structure (Order No. MahaRERA/Secy/129/2024 — PDF):

AccountPurpose
Collection accountReceives all collections from allottees — including parking, amenities and other charges — excluding taxes and statutory dues
Separate account70% of collections transferred here; land + construction cost only
Transaction accountUp to 30% transferred here for the promoter's other project costs

All three must be with a single scheduled bank, opened before applying for registration. They must be free of encumbrance — not escrow, no lien, no loan, no third-party (lender) control.

Why an agent cares: you can tell a buyer exactly where their money goes and why it's protected. That is a genuinely reassuring, genuinely accurate thing to be able to say — and it's a good test of a promoter: ask which bank the three accounts are with.

2.4 Extension of registration (syllabus 6.3) ⚖️ s.6

  • Registration may be extended on application, ordinarily up to one year.
  • The ground is force majeure — war, flood, drought, fire, cyclone, earthquake or other calamity affecting development.
  • The Authority may, in reasonable circumstances and without default by the promoter, extend for reasons recorded in writing.
  • Not available where the promoter is simply late.

🚩 Agent reading: one extension with a stated cause is normal. Three extensions is a pattern, and a pattern is a forecast.

2.5 Revocation of registration (syllabus 6.4) ⚖️ s.7

The Authority may revoke on complaint or suo motu where the promoter:

  • (a) makes default in doing anything required under the Act, rules or regulations;
  • (b) violates any of the terms or conditions of the approval given by the competent authority;
  • (c) is involved in any kind of unfair practice or irregularities — including false/misleading advertisements, and any fraudulent practice.

Minimum 30 days' notice in writing and an opportunity to be heard. Instead of revoking, the Authority may permit registration to continue subject to further terms.

Consequences — s.8. On lapse or revocation the Authority:

  • bars the promoter from accessing the website and lists them as a defaulter;
  • informs other state authorities;
  • may, after consulting the appropriate government, allow the association of allottees to complete the project, or take such other action as it deems fit.

2.7 Quarterly Progress Reports ⏱

Maharashtra runs QPR on a financial-quarter basis: filed within 20 days of quarter end — due by the 20th of July, October, January and April.

The certificate forms:

FormSigned byPurpose
Form 1ArchitectCertifies percentage completion of construction — for withdrawal from the separate account
Form 2Structural EngineerCertifies cost incurred on construction
Form 2A—Quality Assurance Certificate — within three months after each financial year end
Form 3Chartered AccountantCertifies cost incurred on land and construction; withdrawal proportionality
Form 4ArchitectCertificate on completion of the project
Form 5CAAnnual Statement of Accounts — within six months after financial year end

Forms 1, 2 and 3 need not be uploaded every quarter, but must be maintained by the promoter and submitted to the bank to release money from the separate account. Missing a QPR deadline triggers a web-generated notice with 10 days to comply, and non-compliance is displayed publicly on the portal.

3. 🔴 Developer Project Analysis Framework

How an agent decides whether to put a client into a project. Score each dimension 1–5; anything scoring 1 on A, B or F is a stop, not a discount.

A. Legal standing (gate — pass/fail)

  • Registration active, not lapsed or de-registered
  • CC covers the floors being sold
  • Land title clean; if JDA, landowner is a co-promoter
  • No title litigation
  • Proforma agreement uploaded; deviations highlighted

Fail any of these → do not proceed. No price makes this back.

B. Promoter strength

  • Completed projects in the last 5 years, and did they finish on time?
  • Other registrations: any lapsed, de-registered, repeatedly extended?
  • Complaints and adverse orders at Orders & Judgments
  • Is the promoter an SPV? Who stands behind it?
  • Financial signals: is construction finance in place, or is the project funded purely from sales?

C. Delivery credibility

  • Declared completion date vs actual progress
  • Four consecutive QPRs, on time, showing movement
  • Extensions taken and grounds given
  • Site visit: does the physical work match the declared percentage?

D. Product quality

  • Carpet area efficiency (carpet ÷ saleable — see Module 10)
  • Unit layout, floor plate, ventilation, parking ratio
  • Amenities in the sanctioned plan, not just the brochure
  • Specifications in the agreement, not just the show flat

E. Commercial terms

  • Price per carpet sq ft vs comparable projects in the micro-market
  • All-in cost: GST, stamp duty, registration, floor rise, parking, club, maintenance advance
  • Payment plan: is it genuinely construction-linked?
  • Cancellation terms — the model allotment letter caps deduction at 2% (Module 9)

F. Client fit (gate)

  • End-user or investor? Under-construction suits neither automatically
  • Can they carry EMI + rent through a delay?
  • Is the exit horizon realistic for this micro-market?

If the product doesn't fit the client, a good project is still the wrong recommendation.

Then write the five-line note from Module 4. If you can't write it without hedging, you don't understand the project well enough to sell it.

8. Case study 📋

The project that stopped filing.

A Navi Mumbai project registered in 2021, completion declared December 2025. You check in mid-2026:

  • Registration status: active, one extension granted to December 2026
  • QPRs: filed through Q2 FY25, then three consecutive quarters missing
  • Declared completion: 61% — unchanged across the last two filings
  • Complaints: four s.18 complaints filed in the last year
  • Site visit: structure topped out, no finishing work visible, few workers

Your client wants this flat because it's ₹1,100/sq ft cheaper than anything comparable.

Questions: What is actually happening? What are the client's rights if they buy and it stalls? Do you sell this?

Analysis

What's happening. Three missing QPRs plus flat declared progress plus visible site inactivity is the standard signature of a project that has run out of money. The extension bought time, not cash. The four s.18 complaints tell you existing allottees have already concluded the same thing. The ₹1,100/sq ft discount is not a bargain — it is the market pricing this risk, and pricing it roughly correctly.

Rights if they buy and it stalls (s.18). On failure to complete or give possession by the agreed date, the allottee may either:

  • withdraw — full refund of the amount paid with interest at the prescribed rate, plus compensation; or
  • stay — and receive interest for every month of delay until possession.

Compensation is adjudicated by the Adjudicating Officer (s.71). Recovery is as arrears of land revenue (s.40).

But understand what those rights are worth in practice: an order against a promoter with no money is an order, not a payment. RERA gives an excellent remedy; it does not manufacture funds.

Do you sell it? No — not to a buyer who needs to live in it, is stretching to afford it, or would be paying rent and EMI simultaneously. You could discuss it with a cash-rich investor who understands they are underwriting a completion risk and might participate in an allottees' association completing the project under s.8. That is a specialist bet, not a home purchase.

What you actually do: put the QPR gap, the flat progress and the four complaints in writing to the client. Then let them decide with full information. That written note is both good service and your own protection under s.10(c).

Module 7 — Allottees and Their Responsibilities

Official syllabus: MATP Module 7 — 7.1 Rights · 7.2 Obligations · 7.3 Dispute Resolution (7.3.1 Complaints · 7.3.2 Adjudication · 7.3.3 Conciliation · 7.3.4 Appeals) · 7.4 Penal Provisions (7.4.1 Promoters · 7.4.2 Agents · 7.4.3 Allottees · 7.4.4 Offence by companies). Exam weight: 🔴 High — the penal provisions sub-module is heavily examined. Time: 4–5 hours.

1. What I need to learn

What your client is entitled to, what your client owes, and exactly what happens when it goes wrong.

Most agents know rights and forget obligations. The obligations matter: an allottee who stops paying because construction is slow can find themselves in default. Advising a client badly here costs them real money.

2. Simple explanation

2.1 Rights of allottees (syllabus 7.1) ⚖️ s.19

s.19Right
(1)Obtain information on sanctioned plans, layout plans and specifications approved by the competent authority
(2)Know the stage-wise time schedule of completion, including water, sanitation, electricity
(3)Claim possession of the apartment/plot as per the declaration under s.4(2)(l)(C); and the association is entitled to possession of common areas
(4)Claim refund with interest and compensation where the promoter fails to comply or is unable to give possession per the agreement — under s.18
(5)Have documents and plans, including common areas, after possession

And through s.18:

  • If the promoter fails to complete or give possession by the agreed date, the allottee may

withdraw and get a full refund with interest plus compensation; or stay and get interest for every month of delay until possession.

  • If the promoter's title is defective and the allottee suffers loss, compensation is payable and

is not barred by limitation under the Act's scheme.

  • On any structural defect or defect in workmanship, quality or provision of services notified

within 5 years of possession, rectification within 30 days, failing which compensation (s.14(3)).

  • On false advertisement relied upon, withdrawal with full refund + interest (s.12).

2.2 Obligations of allottees (syllabus 7.2) ⚖️ s.19(6)–(11)

s.19Obligation
(6)Make necessary payments in the manner and within the time specified in the agreement — including registration charges, municipal taxes, water and electricity charges, maintenance charges, ground rent and other charges
(7)Be liable to pay interest at the prescribed rate for any delay in payment
(8)The obligations and liability for interest may be reduced by mutual consent of promoter and allottee
(9)Participate in the formation of an association/society/co-operative society
(10)Take physical possession within 2 months of the Occupancy Certificate being issued
(11)Participate in registration of the conveyance deed

🔴 Exam favourite: interest is symmetrical. The promoter pays the allottee for delayed possession; the allottee pays the promoter for delayed payment — at the same prescribed rate under the Maharashtra Rules. Any question that implies only the promoter pays interest is wrong.

🔴 Second favourite: 2 months from OC to take possession — s.19(10). Not 30 days, not 3 months.

7.3.1 Complaints ⚖️ s.31

  • Who: any aggrieved person — an allottee, an association, a promoter, or an agent.
  • Against whom: a promoter, an allottee or an agent — note that agents can be complained against.
  • Where: the Authority, or the Adjudicating Officer.
  • How (Maharashtra): create an account on the MahaRERA complaint portal and file online.

Start at Make a Complaint and Complaint filing under a registered project.

  • ⏱ Fee is prescribed by the Maharashtra Rules — verify the current amount on the portal.

7.3.2 Adjudication ⚖️ s.71

The Adjudicating Officer — a judicial officer appointed by the Authority in consultation with the State Government — decides compensation claims under ss.12, 14, 18 and 19.

The split you must get right:

ForumAwards
The AuthorityOrders, directions, interest, penalty, refund
The Adjudicating OfficerCompensation

s.72 — factors the Adjudicating Officer considers: the amount of disproportionate gain or unfair advantage made as a result of the default; the amount of loss caused as a result of the default; the repetitive nature of the default; and such other factors as may be necessary.

7.3.3 Conciliation ⚖️/💼

MahaRERA operates a Conciliation Forum — a voluntary, without-prejudice settlement mechanism before or alongside adjudication.

  • Both parties must agree to conciliate. Either may decline.
  • Conducted by a conciliation bench, typically drawn from a panel with representation of both

promoter and allottee sides (List of Conciliators).

  • The conciliator does not decide — they help the parties settle. A settlement is recorded.
  • ⏱ Runs on tight timelines: notice within roughly a week, first hearing within about a fortnight,

and matters referred back to MahaRERA if settlement isn't emerging within roughly sixty days. Verify current timelines at Procedure of Conciliation.

  • On the first date of hearing of a complaint, if both parties agree to settle, the matter can be

moved to the Conciliation Forum.

💼 Advise clients honestly: conciliation is fast and preserves the relationship, and it is often the right first move where the promoter is solvent and the dispute is about timing or snagging. It is the wrong move where the promoter has no money — there, you want an order.

7.3.4 Appeals ⚖️ ss.43–44, 58

  • Appeal from an order of the Authority or Adjudicating Officer lies to the **Real Estate

Appellate Tribunal**.

  • Time limit: 60 days from receipt of the order — s.43(5).
  • 🔴 A promoter appealing must first deposit at least 30% of the penalty, or such higher amount

as may be determined, or the total amount payable to the allottee including interest and compensation, whichever is higher.

  • The Tribunal endeavours to dispose of appeals within 60 days.
  • From the Tribunal, appeal lies to the High Court within 60 days, on a question of law

— s.58.

7.4.4 Offences by companies ⚖️ s.69

Where an offence is committed by a company, every person who at the time of the offence was in charge of, and responsible to, the company for the conduct of its business, as well as the company itself, is deemed guilty.

Defences: the person proves the offence was committed without their knowledge or that they exercised all due diligence to prevent it.

Extension: where the offence is proved to have been committed with the consent or connivance of, or is attributable to any neglect on the part of, any director, manager, secretary or other officer, that person is also deemed guilty.

Why an agent cares: if you are registered as a non-individual agent, s.69 reaches the individuals running it. Incorporating does not put a wall between you and RERA liability.

s.70 — compounding. Offences punishable with imprisonment may be compounded by the court, on payment of such sum as prescribed. This is how many matters actually end.

3. 🔴 Realistic scenarios

Scenario A — the 18-month delay

Your client booked a 2BHK in a Mumbai project in 2022. The registered Agreement for Sale states possession by December 2024. It is now June 2026 — 18 months past the agreed date. Your client has paid ₹1.4 crore of a ₹1.75 crore consideration, and is paying ₹98,000/month EMI while also paying ₹55,000/month rent. The promoter has taken one extension and blames a delayed environmental clearance.

Q1. What rights does the buyer have?

Under s.18(1), on the promoter's failure to give possession by the date specified in the agreement, the allottee has two mutually exclusive choices:

  • Withdraw: return of the entire amount paid (₹1.4 crore) with **interest at the prescribed

rate from the date of payment, plus compensation** under s.71; or

  • Stay: interest for every month of delay until handover.

The rate is prescribed by the Maharashtra Rules (commonly expressed as SBI's highest marginal cost of lending rate plus a margin — ⏱ verify the current rate in the Rules, it moves with SBI's MCLR).

Is "delayed environmental clearance" a defence? Generally no. An extension under s.6 is for force majeure — calamity. Approval delays are ordinarily a promoter's own project risk, and the agreed possession date in the registered agreement is what s.18 measures against. The promoter would have to make out a genuine force majeure case.

Q2. What should the agent tell the buyer?

  1. Do the arithmetic before choosing. Withdrawal returns capital plus interest but loses the asset — and if Mumbai prices have moved, ₹1.4 crore + interest may not buy the same flat today. Staying preserves the asset and accrues monthly interest but continues the rent + EMI drain. This is a financial decision, not just a legal one.
  2. Do not stop paying. Withholding instalments puts the client in default under s.19(6)–(7) and hands the promoter a counter-argument. If they want to stop, that decision should follow legal advice, not precede it.
  3. Interest starts from the agreed date, not from when they complain. Delay in filing doesn't forfeit accrued interest, but it does delay recovery.
  4. Consider conciliation first if the promoter is solvent and building — it is faster. Go straight to a s.31 complaint if the site is dead.
  5. Be honest about enforcement. An order is only as good as the promoter's ability to pay. Recovery is as arrears of land revenue (s.40), which is real but slow.

Q3. What documents are needed?

  • Registered Agreement for Sale (the possession date is the anchor)
  • Allotment letter
  • All payment receipts and bank statements
  • Home loan sanction and statement (to evidence EMI outflow)
  • Rent agreement and receipts (for compensation)
  • The project's MahaRERA page, QPR history and extension record — download and date-stamp it
  • All correspondence with the promoter
  • Any advertisement or brochure relied on (relevant if s.12 is in play)

Q4. What is the dispute-resolution process?

  1. Written notice to the promoter recording the delay and the election (withdraw or stay).
  2. Optionally, conciliation via the MahaRERA Conciliation Forum.
  3. Complaint under s.31 to MahaRERA, online.
  4. Authority orders refund/interest/directions; compensation goes to the **Adjudicating Officer** under s.71.
  5. Either party may appeal to the Appellate Tribunal within 60 days — the promoter must pre-deposit ≥30% or the full amount payable, whichever is higher.
  6. High Court on a question of law within 60 days.
  7. Non-compliance by the promoter → s.63 penalties; recovery as arrears of land revenue (s.40).

Scenario B — the buyer who stopped paying

Your client's project is 9 months behind. Frustrated, they stopped paying the last two construction-linked instalments — ₹22 lakh. The promoter has issued a notice threatening cancellation and forfeiture.

The uncomfortable answer: the client is now in default under s.19(6), and is liable for interest at the prescribed rate under s.19(7). Delay by the promoter does not automatically suspend the allottee's payment obligation — the Act gives the allottee a remedy (s.18), not a self-help right to withhold.

Better advice, in order: pay under protest while recording the delay in writing; claim s.18 interest for the promoter's delay separately; and if cancellation is threatened, note that forfeiture terms are constrained — the model allotment letter caps the deduction on cancellation at 2% (Module 9). Never advise a client to stop paying as a negotiating tactic.

Scenario C — the defect that appeared in year four

Three years and eight months after possession, seepage appears through an external wall in your client's flat. The promoter says the warranty was one year.

s.14(3) governs, not the promoter's warranty. Any structural defect or defect in workmanship, quality or provision of services brought to the promoter's notice within 5 years of possession must be rectified within 30 days, free of charge. Failing that, the allottee is entitled to compensation.

Action: written notice to the promoter, dated, with photographs; keep proof of delivery (the 5-year clock and the 30-day clock both depend on that date). If unrectified in 30 days, complain under s.31; compensation via s.71.

8. Case study 📋 — the OC that arrived quietly

Your client's project receives its Occupancy Certificate on 3 March. The promoter emails a possession notice to the address on file — an old email your client no longer checks. On 20 June, the promoter demands maintenance charges and municipal taxes from 3 March, plus interest on delayed payment of the final instalment, and points to s.19(10).

Questions: Is the promoter right? What is the client's exposure? What should have happened?

Analysis

The promoter is substantially right on the framework. Under s.19(10) the allottee must take physical possession within 2 months of the OC being issued — here, by 3 May. Under s.19(6) the allottee is liable for municipal taxes, maintenance and other outgoings as specified in the agreement, and under s.19(7) interest runs on delayed payments. Once a valid possession offer is made following OC, liability for outgoings typically shifts to the allottee — this is standard and is usually spelled out in the Agreement for Sale.

But three things are contestable:

  1. Was the possession offer validly served? A notice to a stale email may not be effective service. What does the agreement say about the mode of notice? That clause decides this.
  2. Was the OC valid for this unit? A part-OC not covering the client's wing or floor is not an OC for this purpose.
  3. Was the flat actually ready to be possessed? An offer of possession of a unit with incomplete work or without services is arguably not a valid offer at all.

Client exposure: roughly 3.5 months of maintenance and municipal taxes, plus interest on the final instalment — real money, and largely avoidable.

What should have happened — and this is the agent's job: after the Agreement for Sale, you should have (a) ensured the promoter had current contact details, (b) told the client the 2-month clock exists, and (c) set your own reminder to watch for the OC. This is exactly the "After the transaction" section of the Module 5 compliance checklist. A five-minute follow-up would have saved the client a few lakh rupees — and it is the kind of thing that generates referrals for the next decade.

3. The three cross-checks that catch most problems

Most defects surface from three comparisons. Do these every single time.

① Brochure vs sanctioned plan. Towers, floors, unit mix, amenities. Anything in the brochure and not in the plan does not exist yet.

② CC floor coverage vs the unit's floor. The single most under-checked item in Mumbai new-build sales.

③ Carpet area — disclosure vs cost sheet vs draft agreement. All three must state the same number. If they don't, you've found either an error or a practice.

3. The documents

3.1 Allotment Letter (syllabus 9.1)

What it is → The promoter's written confirmation that a specific unit is allotted to a specific buyer, on stated terms, on receipt of the booking amount. It is the first binding document in most transactions.

Who signs → Promoter and allottee.

When used → At booking, before the Agreement for Sale.

Important clauses →

  • Unit identification: tower, floor, unit number
  • RERA carpet area — must match the disclosure
  • Total consideration and the breakdown of other charges
  • Payment schedule
  • Cancellation and forfeiture terms

  • That an Agreement for Sale will be executed and registered

3.1.1 The Model Allotment Letter ⚖️/⏱

MahaRERA prescribed a model allotment letter and required promoters to upload their proforma (the s.4(2)(g) obligation). Key features:

  • It sets a minimum period within which the allottee may cancel the booking.
  • It caps the penalty the promoter may deduct at 2% of the consideration on cancellation by

the allottee, or on non-payment of the balance booking amount/advance.

  • The promoter may amend the model only in the allottee's favour — i.e. only to increase the

cancellation window or decrease the deduction percentage. Not otherwise.

  • Deviations from the model proforma must be highlighted in a different colour and uploaded.

🔴 This is a genuine, concrete, client-facing thing you know that most agents don't: a buyer who cancels cannot lawfully be charged more than 2% under the model letter. Agents routinely watch clients lose 10% because nobody told them.

⏱ Verify the current model and its terms at MahaRERA — Resources for Homebuyers and the Orders index.

3.2 Model Form of Agreement for Sale (syllabus 9.2) ⚖️

Source of the obligation: s.13 of the Act + Rule 10 and Annexure A of the Maharashtra RERA Rules, 2017.

What it is → The principal contract of sale for an under-construction unit. Rule 10 prescribes the model form at Annexure A.

Who signs → Promoter and allottee (and the landowner/confirming party where relevant).

When used → Before more than 10% of the cost is taken — and it must be registered.

How much may be changed: the promoter may modify Annexure A, provided the agreement remains in conformity with s.13(2) and the rules and regulations. But:

  • Clauses that are mandatory under the Act must be retained in every agreement.
  • Provisions contrary to statutory conditions are not binding on the parties.
  • Deviations/modifications must be highlighted and uploaded with a deviation sheet.

Clauses to read every time:

3.3 Conveyance Deed / Sale Deed

What it is → The instrument that transfers title. The Agreement for Sale creates a right to obtain conveyance; the conveyance actually conveys.

When used → On completion — to the individual allottee for a plot/independent unit, and to the association of allottees for the land and building under s.17.

Why it matters → Where a promoter fails to convey, Maharashtra provides for deemed conveyance — a route for societies to obtain title without the promoter's cooperation. This is a recurring Mumbai problem worth knowing exists.

4. TDS (syllabus 9.3) ⏱

Two different TDS provisions touch a property transaction. Do not confuse them.

4.1 TDS on purchase of an apartment — Section 194-IA

ItemPosition
Who deductsThe buyer
Rate1% of the consideration
ThresholdConsideration ₹50 lakh or more
On whatThe sale consideration (and, per current practice, measured against stamp duty value)
FormChallan-cum-statement Form 26QB; certificate Form 16B to the seller

Worked example: flat at ₹1.20 crore. TDS = 1% × ₹1,20,00,000 = ₹1,20,000. The buyer pays ₹1,18,80,000 to the seller and ₹1,20,000 to the government via Form 26QB.

🚩 The classic error: buyers deduct 1% only on the amount above ₹50 lakh. Wrong — once the threshold is crossed, TDS applies to the whole consideration.

10. Case study 📋 — the ₹42 lakh booking

Your client books a ₹3.1 crore flat in Andheri. The developer asks for ₹42 lakh as "booking and allotment" — about 13.5% — against an allotment letter only, with the Agreement for Sale "at the time of the 3rd slab, in about five months". The allotment letter says 10% will be forfeited on cancellation.

Questions: What's wrong, how much, and what do you do?

Analysis

Two distinct breaches.

① The 10% cap — s.13(1). The promoter may not accept more than 10% of the cost as advance or application fee without first entering into a registered Agreement for Sale. 10% of ₹3.1 crore is ₹31 lakh. The demand of ₹42 lakh exceeds it by ₹11 lakh. Taking that money without a registered AFS contravenes s.13, attracting penalty under s.61 (up to 5% of the estimated project cost).

Note the two limbs people miss: the agreement must be entered into and registered. A signed-but-unregistered agreement does not unlock the >10% position.

② The forfeiture clause. MahaRERA's model allotment letter caps the deduction at 2% on cancellation by the allottee, and permits promoters to depart from the model only in the allottee's favour. A 10% forfeiture clause is a deviation against the allottee. On a ₹3.1 crore flat that's the difference between ₹6.2 lakh and ₹31 lakh — a ₹24.8 lakh exposure your client would otherwise never know about.

What you do:

  1. Tell the client, in writing, both points with the section and the order behind each.
  2. Ask the developer to either cap the booking at ₹31 lakh, or execute and register the AFS now.
  3. Ask for the forfeiture clause to be brought to 2%, and point to the uploaded proforma and deviation sheet on the MahaRERA record — if their uploaded proforma says 2% and the letter they handed your client says 10%, that discrepancy is a strong negotiating fact.
  4. If they refuse both: document the advice, and let the client decide with full information.

Your own exposure: facilitating this without raising it engages s.10(c) (unfair trade practice / misrepresentation by omission) and s.10(d) (failing to facilitate the information the allottee is entitled to). The written note protects your client and you.

The wider point: the 10% rule and the 2% cap are the two most commercially valuable things in this entire module. Between them they are worth more to a client than any negotiation on base price.

🏠 The master example — one flat, all the maths

Everything below runs through a single flat so the numbers connect.

Flat A — Goregaon East, Mumbai. Under construction. RERA carpet area: 750 sq ft · Saleable quoted: 1,050 sq ft · Quoted rate: ₹20,000/sq ft on saleable Agreement value: 1,050 × ₹20,000 = ₹2,10,00,000

1. Carpet area and loading

Concept → Under s.2(k) you must sell on carpet area. "Saleable" or "super built-up" is a marketing number with no statutory definition. Loading is the gap.

Formula →

Loading %  =  (Saleable area − Carpet area) ÷ Carpet area × 100
Effective rate on carpet  =  Agreement value ÷ Carpet area

Example → Loading = (1,050 − 750) ÷ 750 × 100 = 40% Effective rate = ₹2,10,00,000 ÷ 750 = ₹28,000 per carpet sq ft

Mumbai reality 💼 → Loading commonly runs 30–45%. Two flats quoted at "₹20,000/sq ft" with loadings of 30% and 45% differ by roughly ₹3,000 per carpet sq ft in real terms. Always re-quote on carpet before comparing anything.

⚠️ 60 sq m = 645.8 sq ft. Keep that conversion handy — the GST affordable-housing test uses square metres of carpet area.

Practice → Flat B: carpet 620 sq ft, saleable 930 sq ft, agreement value ₹1,58,10,000. Loading? Effective carpet rate? Which is better value, A or B?

Answer

Loading = (930−620)/620 = 50%. Effective = 1,58,10,000 ÷ 620 = ₹25,500/carpet sq ft. B is better value on carpet (₹25,500 vs ₹28,000) despite the far worse loading — which is exactly why you compare on carpet, not on loading and not on the quoted rate.

2. Stamp duty ⏱ (syllabus 10.1.4)

Concept → A state tax on the instrument, payable to Maharashtra, levied on the higher of the agreement value and the ready reckoner (ASR) value.

⏱ Mumbai rates (illustrative — verify at IGR):

BuyerBaseMetro cessTotal
Male / joint (with a male)5%1%6%
Female sole owner4%1%5%

The 1% Metro Cess funds metro rail expansion and applies within Mumbai municipal limits. The women's concession applies where a woman is the sole owner; joint ownership with a male co-owner attracts the blended rate.

Formula → Stamp duty = Applicable % × HIGHER of (Agreement value, ASR value)

Example →

  • Male buyer: 6% × ₹2,10,00,000 = ₹12,60,000
  • Female sole buyer: 5% × ₹2,10,00,000 = ₹10,50,000 (saves ₹2,10,000)

The ready reckoner trap 🚩 → If the ASR value is ₹2,25,00,000 while the agreement value is ₹2,10,00,000, stamp duty is charged on ₹2,25,00,000: 6% × 2,25,00,000 = ₹13,50,000 — ₹90,000 more than the buyer budgeted. Always check e-ASR before quoting stamp duty.

Practice → Female sole buyer, agreement value ₹95,00,000, ASR value ₹1,02,00,000. Stamp duty?

Answer

Higher value = ₹1,02,00,000. 5% × 1,02,00,000 = ₹5,10,000.

3. Registration charge ⏱ (syllabus 10.1.3)

Concept → The fee for registering the instrument under the Registration Act, 1908.

⏱ Maharashtra (illustrative):

Property value > ₹30 lakh  →  ₹30,000 (capped)
Property value ≤ ₹30 lakh  →  1% of value

Example → Flat A: value ₹2.1 crore > ₹30 lakh → ₹30,000

Practice → Agreement value ₹28,00,000. Registration charge?

Answer1% × 28,00,000 = **₹28,000** (below the ₹30 lakh threshold, so 1% applies, not the cap).

4. GST ⏱ (syllabus 10.1.1)

Concept → GST applies only to under-construction property sold by a promoter. A completed unit with an Occupancy Certificate is outside GST entirely — it's a transfer of immovable property.

⏱ Rates (illustrative — verify at CBIC):

CategoryRateITC
Affordable residential1%No ITC

Affordable housing test — both conditions must hold:

  • Consideration ≤ ₹45 lakh, and
  • Carpet area ≤ 60 sq m in metros (≤ 90 sq m non-metro). Mumbai is a metro.

ITC note: Input Tax Credit is not available to residential buyers — blocked under s.17(5)(d) of the CGST Act. Even a GST-registered business buyer cannot claim the 1% or 5% back.

Example → Flat A: 750 sq ft = 69.7 sq m (>60) and ₹2.1 crore (>₹45 lakh) → not affordable. GST = 5% × ₹2,10,00,000 = ₹10,50,000

Practice → Flat in Mumbai: carpet 58 sq m, agreement value ₹43,00,000. GST?

Answer

Both tests pass (≤60 sq m and ≤₹45 lakh) → 1% = ₹43,000. If either failed, it would be 5%.

5. TDS ⏱ (syllabus 10.1.2)

5.1 On the purchase — s.194-IA

1% of consideration where consideration is ₹50 lakh or more. Buyer deducts, files Form 26QB, issues Form 16B. On the whole amount, not the excess over ₹50 lakh.

Example → 1% × ₹2,10,00,000 = ₹2,10,000 The buyer pays ₹2,07,90,000 to the promoter and ₹2,10,000 to the government.

This is not an extra cost. It is part of the consideration, routed to the government. Agents confuse buyers constantly on this point.

5.2 On brokerage — s.194H ⏱

2% (from 1 Oct 2024), threshold ₹20,000 per financial year, 20% without PAN.

Example — your own fee on Flat A at 2% brokerage:

6. External Development Charges (syllabus 10.1.5) 💼

Concept → Charges levied by a development/planning authority to fund external infrastructure — roads, water, sewerage, drainage — outside the project boundary. Related levies include Infrastructure Development Charges, betterment charges, premium FSI charges and open-space deficiency charges.

Important Maharashtra note: "EDC" as a named, standardised levy is most prominent in states like Haryana. In Maharashtra and especially Greater Mumbai, the equivalent burden appears as development charges, premiums (including premium FSI), and various planning-authority levies under the MRTP Act and DCPR 2034, varying by authority — MCGM, MMRDA, CIDCO, and municipal corporations in the MMR.

What matters to you: these are developer costs that end up in your buyer's price, and they sometimes appear as separate line items in a cost sheet. If a cost sheet has an "infrastructure charge" or "development charge" line, ask what it is and whether it is already in the agreement value or on top. ⏱ Rates are authority-specific — verify with the planning authority.

             (1 + r)^n − 1

Practice → P = ₹80,00,000, 9% p.a., 15 years. Estimate the EMI.

Answer

8.3 FOIR 💼

FOIR = All monthly obligations ÷ Net monthly income. Lenders typically cap around 50–60%. An EMI of ₹1,36,700 at a 55% FOIR implies a net monthly income around ₹2.5 lakh — before any other loan. Check this at qualification.

9. Investment metrics

9.1 Rental yield

Gross yield = Annual rent ÷ Total acquisition cost × 100
Net yield   = (Annual rent − maintenance − property tax − vacancy) ÷ Total acquisition cost × 100

Example → Flat A rents at ₹65,000/month → ₹7,80,000/year. Gross (on all-in ₹2,39,40,000) = 3.26% · Gross (on agreement value) = 3.71% Net, after ₹90,000 outgoings = 2.88%

💼 Mumbai residential gross yields typically run ~2.5–3.5%. Commercial runs materially higher. A client told to expect 6% residential yield in Mumbai has been misled — and if you said it, you have a s.10(c) problem.

9.2 Capital appreciation and CAGR

CAGR = ((Final ÷ Initial)^(1 ÷ years) − 1) × 100

Example → All-in ₹2,39,40,000; sold after 5 years at ₹2,95,00,000. Ratio 1.2322 → CAGR ≈ 4.27%

9.3 ROI

Simple ROI = (Sale price − Total acquisition cost) ÷ Total acquisition cost × 100

= (2,95,00,000 − 2,39,40,000) ÷ 2,39,40,000 = 23.2% over 5 years

🚩 The honest version: this ignores the interest paid, the rent received, brokerage on exit, and capital gains tax. Comparing a gross sale price against an all-in cost is the most common way property returns get overstated. Total return = rental income + appreciation − all costs − financing − tax.

Practice → Bought all-in ₹1,20,00,000, sold at ₹1,85,00,000 after 7 years. CAGR? Simple ROI?

Answer

Ratio = 1.5417. CAGR = 1.5417^(1/7) − 1 ≈ 6.4% p.a. Simple ROI = 65,00,000/1,20,00,000 = 54.2% over 7 years.

Full working

3. FSI, TDR and land economics 🔴

This is the section that separates people who understand Mumbai from people who just live here.

3.1 FSI (Floor Space Index)

FSI = Total permissible built-up area ÷ Plot area

A 1,000 sq m plot with FSI 3.0 permits 3,000 sq m of built-up area.

Under DCPR 2034, permissible FSI is linked to road width — taller buildings only where the road can carry the traffic. The regulations sort residential and commercial plots into five road-width bands, each carrying a fixed quantum of premium FSI and TDR on top of the basic zonal FSI. Indicatively, permissible FSI runs from about 1.00 (a suburban plot on a road under 9 m) to 3.00 (an Island City plot on a road of 27 m or more) — before any 33-series incentive is added.

⏱ Verify current bands and quanta in DCPR 2034 itself — they are amended periodically.

3.2 TDR (Transferable Development Rights)

When a landowner's plot is acquired for a public purpose — a road, a school, a garden — they may be compensated not in cash but in development rights usable elsewhere. TDR is bought, sold and "loaded" onto a receiving plot within the rules.

Why you care: TDR is a traded commodity with a price. Its cost is a real input into a developer's feasibility, and when TDR prices move, project economics move with them.

3.3 The 33-series — Mumbai's redevelopment engine

Mumbai builds mostly by rebuilding. The 33-series of DCPR 2034 grants incentive FSI to make that viable.

3.4 The economics of a redevelopment 💼

A society redevelopment has a simple logic:

Total buildable area  =  REHAB component (free, to existing members)
                       + SALE component (the developer's revenue)

Developer's economics: SALE revenue − (rehab construction + sale construction
                       + rent/corpus to members + premiums + TDR + finance) = margin

What this means for you as an agent:

  • The sale component is the inventory you'll be selling.
  • The project is only viable if incentive FSI is enough. **When a developer says "the numbers don't

work", they mean the sale component is too small relative to the rehab obligation.**

  • Members receiving a corpus and rent during construction is normal.
  • 🚩 The chronic risk: societies signing with a developer who cannot fund the rehab, leaving members

out of their homes and out of rent. Mumbai has many of these. When you sell in a redevelopment, check who the society is and whether the members are being paid on time — that is a live indicator of the developer's cash position.

9. Case study 📋 — the Navi Mumbai question

A client has ₹1.6 crore. They can buy a compact 2BHK in Chembur (established, ready, immediate rental) or a larger 3BHK in Ulwe (under construction, near the airport and Atal Setu). They ask: "Which will appreciate more?"

A professional answer is not a prediction. It's a structure:

Ulwe — the case for. The infrastructure thesis is real and unusually concrete: an operating airport, Atal Setu, metro. Navi Mumbai leads MMR new-supply share. Lower entry price means a given absolute price rise is a larger percentage return.

Ulwe — the case against. Supply is the risk. Growth corridors attract developers, and MMR is producing tens of thousands of new units a quarter with national unsold inventory rising. In a corridor with abundant developable land, new supply can absorb demand growth and cap price growth for years. Rental demand also lags physical infrastructure, often by several years — so the early years may deliver poor yield and no appreciation simultaneously. Plus: completion risk.

Chembur — the case for. Established micro-market, constrained supply, existing rental demand from day one, no construction risk, immediately mortgageable and immediately sellable. Liquidity is a return. The eastern suburbs have benefited from the Eastern Freeway and SCLR.

Chembur — the case against. Lower percentage upside; already priced for its advantages.

What you actually tell them — three questions, not an answer:

  1. What's the holding period? Under 5 years → established market. 10+ years → the growth corridor thesis has time to work.
  2. Do they need income now? Chembur rents from month one. Ulwe rents in ~3 years, at a lower yield.
  3. Can they carry both EMI and rent through an 18-month delay, plus a possible further delay? If not, under-construction is not suitable regardless of the thesis.

And be honest about the limit of your knowledge: you can describe supply, infrastructure and absorption. You cannot forecast prices, and any agent who says they can is selling, not advising. Saying so out loud is what makes the rest of your advice credible.

Module 12 — Real Estate Finance

📌 Beyond the MATP syllabus. Not examinable. This is the module that lets you understand what the person across the table is actually optimising for. Time: 5–6 hours. Build the model — don't just read it.

1. What I need to learn

How a developer makes money, where the risk sits, and why they behave the way they do — why they launch before they're ready, why they discount at quarter-end, why they resist changing a payment plan, why "the numbers don't work."

Once you understand a developer's cash flow, their negotiating behaviour stops being mysterious.

2. Developer economics — the basic equation

GDV (Gross Development Value)  =  Saleable area × Average realisation
Total Project Cost             =  Land + Construction + Approvals/Premiums
                                  + Marketing/Brokerage + Finance + Overheads
Development Profit             =  GDV − Total Project Cost
Development Margin             =  Profit ÷ GDV
Return on Cost                 =  Profit ÷ Total Project Cost

The residual land value method — how a developer decides what land is worth:

Residual Land Value = GDV − (all costs except land) − required developer profit

This is why land prices in a micro-market track achievable sale prices with a lag. Land is the residual, not the driver.

Everything after launch is a race to convert inventory into cash fast enough to service finance. That is why developers would rather give you a bigger brokerage than cut the headline price — the headline price sets the benchmark for every remaining unit and for the lender's valuation; your commission is a one-off.

4. The metrics

4.1 NPV

NPV = Σ [ Cash flow_t ÷ (1 + r)^t ]

Positive NPV at your required return = the project creates value. r is the discount rate — for Indian residential development, equity discount rates of 15–20% are common. ⏱

4.2 IRR

The discount rate at which NPV = 0. IRR is time-sensitive — the same profit earned two years later is a much lower IRR. This is why delay destroys developer returns faster than cost overruns.

4.3 Break-even

Break-even sales (sq ft) = Total Project Cost ÷ Average realisation per sq ft

4.4 Sales velocity and absorption

Sales velocity = Units sold ÷ Month
Absorption rate = Units sold ÷ Total units available
Quarters-to-sell = Unsold inventory ÷ Average quarterly sales

Sales velocity is the number a developer watches daily. It determines whether collections outpace construction spend — and whether they can service debt.

3. Reading a market report properly 🔴

Five questions, every time:

  1. What is the geography? "Mumbai" in one report means MMR; in another it means Greater Mumbai only. Navi Mumbai and Thane may or may not be included. Check the definition before comparing.
  2. What is the segment? Weighted averages across a market with luxury and affordable in it are nearly meaningless. Look for the segment split.
  3. Is "price" a listing price, a transaction price, or an index? Listing prices overstate. RBI's HPI is built from registration data and is the most neutral.
  4. What's the base period? Year-on-year against a weak base flatters everything. RBI rebased its HPI to 2022-23 — comparisons across the rebasing need care.
  5. Who paid for it? Consultancy research is high quality and also marketing. It is rarely wrong, but it is rarely bearish either. Triangulate.

💼 The professional habit: never quote a single source. Take Knight Frank and ANAROCK and JLL on the same quarter. Where they agree, you can speak confidently. Where they diverge, that divergence is itself the interesting fact — and saying so is far more impressive than a confident wrong number.

5. 🎯 Practical assignment: analyse one Mumbai micro-market

This is the capstone of the whole course. Budget 4–6 hours.

Write down exactly what you're including. Pin nodes: which stations, which roads. Every number you produce afterwards must refer to this boundary.

Step 2 — the supply pipeline (60 min) 🏛

On MahaRERA, search registered projects in that taluka/village. Record: number of registered projects, their declared completion dates, total registered units where visible, and how many are lapsed or de-registered. This is your forward supply pipeline.

  • Asking prices: sample at least 10 live listings. Convert every one to ₹ per carpet sq ft (Module 10)
  • Neutral series: the latest RBI HPI reading for Mumbai

Report a range, not a point. A single "average price" for a micro-market is almost always misleading.

Step 5 — rental market (30 min)

Sample 10 rental listings for a comparable 2BHK. Compute gross yield against your median capital value. Compare with the ~2.5–3.5% Mumbai residential norm.

Step 6 — infrastructure (45 min) 🏛

List every infrastructure project affecting the boundary, with status and expected completion, from MMRDA / MCGM / metro authority sources. Distinguish announced from under construction from commissioned — they price completely differently.

Step 7 — developers (30 min) 🏛

Identify the three most active promoters. For each, pull their MahaRERA track record: completed projects, delays, extensions, complaints.

Step 8 — write it (60 min)

Two pages maximum:

  1. What this micro-market is, and who buys here
  2. Supply pipeline
  3. Demand and absorption
  4. Price range on carpet, and rental yield
  5. Infrastructure, with timing
  6. The three main developers
  7. Your view — and separately, what would change your mind
  8. Sources, with dates

The discipline that makes it professional

Mark every statement:

  • [F] Fact — sourced, with a link and a date
  • [I] Inference — reasoning from facts
  • [O] Opinion — your judgement

Most agents produce pure [O] and present it as [F]. Doing this properly, once, will make you noticeably better than the market.

6. Case study 📋 — two reports, one market, different answers

Report A says a micro-market's prices rose 9% YoY. Report B says 3%. Both are reputable and cover the same quarter. Your client asks which is right.

The answer is almost always: both, and the difference is definitional. Work through it:

  1. Geography. Does A cover a wider area including a premium pocket that pulled the average up?
  2. Segment. Is A a weighted average across all launches while B tracks a fixed basket? If luxury launches dominated the quarter, a weighted average rises without any individual flat getting more expensive. This is the single most common cause.
  3. Price definition. Listing/asking prices (A) vs registered transaction values (B)? Asking prices run ahead in a rising market and lag in a falling one.
  4. Base period. Is one comparing against a COVID-depressed base?
  5. Method. Simple average vs median vs hedonic index. Averages are dragged by outliers.

What you say to the client:

"They're measuring different things. A is a weighted average of new launches across a wider area, which rises when the mix shifts to premium product. B tracks registered transactions in a narrower basket. For your decision — you're buying a specific 2BHK in a specific building — the number that matters is neither: it's the registered transaction values for comparable units in that building over the last six months, which we can pull from IGR. Let me do that."

That answer is worth more than either report. It shows you understand what the numbers are, you're not selling a narrative, and you know where the decision-relevant data actually lives.
